## OWNERSHIP (political control, Feb 1763)

### Spain — Viceroyalty of New Spain
- Extent: core Mexico; Captaincy-General of Guatemala (Chiapas→Costa Rica); northern frontier (Nueva Vizcaya, Nuevo León, Nuevo Santander, Texas, Nuevo México); Caribbean (Cuba, Santo Domingo/E. Hispaniola, Puerto Rico); the Philippines (via Manila galleon). NEW 1763: Louisiana west of the Mississippi + New Orleans (from France; formal handover 1769).
- Capital: Mexico City. Key cities: Puebla, Guanajuato, Zacatecas, Veracruz, Havana, Guatemala (Antigua).
- Subject/overlord: viceroyalty of the Spanish Crown; Guatemala + Cuba subordinate captaincies-general.
- Source: Kuethe & Andrien 2014; WORLD_Americas §II [MONOGRAPH]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: Alta California NOT colonized (first mission San Diego 1769); no independent Mexico/Central America (1821); Louisiana admin still transitional (French locals in place).

### Spain — Viceroyalty of Peru
- Extent: core Peru (Audiencias of Lima, Cuzco); Upper Peru / Audiencia of Charcas (modern Bolivia, incl. Potosí); Captaincy-General of Chile; **Río de la Plata region (modern Argentina, Paraguay, Uruguay) governed from Lima**; Audiencia of Quito (Ecuador, under Peru 1751–1802).
- Capital: Lima. Key cities: Cuzco, Potosí, Charcas/Sucre, Arequipa, Buenos Aires (subordinate), Asunción, Quito.
- Source: Fisher *Bourbon Peru* 2003; WORLD_Americas §II.A [MONOGRAPH]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: **Viceroyalty of Río de la Plata does NOT exist (created 1776)** — Argentina/Paraguay/Uruguay are under Peru/Lima in 1763.

### Spain — Viceroyalty of New Granada
- Extent: core Nueva Granada (modern Colombia, Audiencia of Bogotá); Captaincy-General of Venezuela (loosely organized under Bogotá — the unified C-G is 1777); Panama (Audiencia of Panamá). (Quito is under Peru in 1763, not New Granada.)
- Capital: Santafé de Bogotá. Key cities: Cartagena, Panamá, Caracas, Maracaibo, Popayán.
- Source: Kuethe & Andrien 2014; WORLD_Americas §II.A.3 [MONOGRAPH]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: no independent Gran Colombia/Colombia/Venezuela/Ecuador; CRT independent Cartagena republic (~1811) is anachronistic — must be New Granada (SFB).

### Portugal — State of Brazil (+ State of Maranhão e Grão-Pará)
- Extent: Atlantic Brazil. Northeast sugar (Bahia/Salvador, Pernambuco/Recife); center (Minas Gerais gold, Rio, São Paulo); north (Pará/Maranhão, separate administrative state until 1774, cap. Belém); contested southern frontier (Santa Catarina, Rio Grande, Colônia do Sacramento on the Plata).
- Capital: **Rio de Janeiro (moved from Salvador in 1763 — the exact start year)**. Key cities: Salvador, Recife, Vila Rica/Ouro Preto, São Paulo, Belém.
- Subject/overlord: colony of the Portuguese Crown under a Viceroy (Conde da Cunha, 1763–67).
- Source: Maxwell *Pombal* 1995; Boxer; WORLD_Americas §III [MONOGRAPH]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: no independent Brazil (1822); Jesuit missions dissolved (Jesuits expelled 1759); gold past peak; interior Amazon is Indigenous, not Portuguese-controlled.

### Britain — Thirteen Colonies
- Extent: Atlantic seaboard — New England (Mass. incl. Maine, NH, RI, CT), Middle (NY, NJ, PA, DE), South (MD, VA, NC, SC, GA). Settlement barred west of the Appalachians by the Royal Proclamation of 1763.
- Capital/key cities: (no capital) Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Charleston, Baltimore.
- Subject/overlord: British colonies (NO United States — independence 1776).
- Source: Anderson *Crucible of War* 2000; Royal Proclamation 1763 [PRIMARY]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: NO USA tag; NO settlement in trans-Appalachia/Ohio (Proclamation Line + Pontiac) — that interior is Native.

### Britain — Canada / Quebec + Maritimes (NEW 1763)
- Extent: Province of Quebec (St. Lawrence: Montreal, Quebec City, Trois-Rivières) ceded by France; Nova Scotia/Acadia (British since 1713); Newfoundland; Rupert's Land (HBC charter). Pays d'en Haut (Great Lakes forts) + Illinois Country east of Mississippi nominally British but Native-dominated (Pontiac's War, May 1763).
- Capital/key: Quebec City, Halifax.
- NOTE (mod handling): at the exact Feb 1763 snapshot the cession is days old; French-Canadian (canadien/quebecois) population and Catholic religion remain in place. French garrison towns Detroit, Kaskaskia, Vincennes still culturally French.
- Source: Anderson 2000; Calloway 2006 [MONOGRAPH]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: no independent/loyalist Ontario settler wave (post-1783); Detroit/Kaskaskia/St.Louis handled below.

### Britain — West Indies
- Extent: Jamaica, Barbados, Leeward Is. (Antigua, St Kitts, Nevis, Montserrat), Bahamas (British since 1718); NEW 1763 "Ceded Islands" — Dominica, St Vincent, Grenada, Tobago. British Honduras (Belize) = tolerated logwood cutters on nominally Spanish coast.
- Source: Dunn *Sugar and Slaves* 1972; Treaty of Paris [MONOGRAPH/PRIMARY]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: **Trinidad is SPANISH until 1797** (not British in 1763); **St. Lucia is FRENCH** (British only from 1814).

### France — Caribbean + Guiana (remnants)
- Extent: Saint-Domingue (W. Hispaniola, cap. Cap-Français) — wealthiest colony in the hemisphere; Guadeloupe & Martinique (returned by Britain 1763); French Guiana (cap. Cayenne); St-Pierre & Miquelon (fishing).
- Source: Burnard & Garrigus *Plantation Machine* 2016; Treaty of Paris [MONOGRAPH/PRIMARY]
- ANACHRONISM FLAGS: no independent Haiti (1804) — Saint-Domingue is French, enslaved-majority.

### Netherlands & Denmark — Caribbean/Guiana
- Dutch: Suriname (cap. Paramaribo), Curaçao/Aruba/Bonaire, St Eustatius (smuggling hub), Saba, S. St Maarten. Danish: St Thomas, St John, St Croix.
- NOTE: **Demerara/Essequibo/Berbice (Georgetown, Guyana) are DUTCH in 1763** (British only from 1796).
- Source: WORLD_Americas §VI [MONOGRAPH-derived]

### INDEPENDENT / unconquered zones (NOT European-controlled in 1763)
- **Comanche** (Numunuu) — dominant power of the southern Plains (TX/OK/NM/KS), extracting tribute from Spanish NM & Texas. Hämäläinen 2008 [MONOGRAPH].
- **Sioux/Lakota-Dakota** — expanding across northern Plains (Dakotas/MT/MN/WY). Hämäläinen 2019 [MONOGRAPH].
- **Pontiac's Confederacy & pays d'en haut** — Ottawa/Ojibwe/Potawatomi/Wyandot/Seneca/Delaware/Shawnee/Miami; open war on British forts from May 1763. Dowd 2002 [MONOGRAPH].
- **Iroquois/Haudenosaunee Six Nations** — NY/PA, weakened but a diplomatic power.
- **Osage** — effective power of the lower Missouri valley (nominal Spanish Louisiana). Osage ~6–7k.
- **Cherokee** (S. Appalachians, post-Anglo-Cherokee War 1758–61) & **Creek Confederacy** (GA/AL/FL) — autonomous.
- **Mapuche / Araucanía** — INDEPENDENT south of the Bío-Bío; War of Arauco ongoing, frontier by *parlamento*.
- **Gran Chaco peoples** (Guaycurú/Wichí) & **Patagonia** (Tehuelche) — unconquered.
- **Amazon interior** — hundreds of Indigenous nations; Portuguese presence limited to lower Amazon + missions.
- **Alaska / Russian America** — NASCENT: no permanent Russian settlement until Kodiak 1784; Russian America Co. 1799. In 1763 the coast is Indigenous (Tlingit, Alutiiq/Sugpiaq, Aleut, Yup'ik). Should NOT be a held Russian territory.

## OWNERSHIP (political control, Feb 1763)

### GREAT BRITAIN
**Territorial extent**: England, Wales, Scotland, Ireland (entire island under British control post-1691 Williamite War).  
**Capital**: London  
**Key cities**: Edinburgh, Dublin, Bristol, Liverpool, Manchester, Birmingham  
**Subject/overlord/personal-union**: Hanover in personal union (George III both King of Britain and Elector of Hanover; governed via Privy Council of Hanover & German Chancery in St James's Palace; George III never visited Hanover).  
**Source**: Black 2001 [MONOGRAPH]; Wikipedia [WEAK] personal-union mechanics.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**: None. Acts of Union 1707 united England-Wales with Scotland; Ireland under English/British rule since 1691.

### HANOVER (Electorate of Brunswick-Lüneburg)
**Territorial extent**: Principality of Calenberg, Göttingen, Grubenhagen, Lüneburg (electoral dignity 1692).  
**Capital**: Hanover  
**Overlord**: Personal union with Great Britain (George III, 1760–1820).  
**Governance**: Privy Council of Hanover (*Geheimer Rat*) held executive authority; German Chancery (*Deutsche Kanzlei*) in London transmitted memoranda. Premiere Minister 1753–1770: Gerlach Adolph, Baron von Münchhausen.  
**Source**: Wikipedia [WEAK]; constitutional structure.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**: None. Personal union ends 1837 (Salic Law separation upon Victoria's accession).

### FRANCE (Bourbon)
**Territorial extent**: Roughly modern France minus Corsica (Genoese until 1768), minus Lorraine (autonomous under Stanisław Leszczyński until 1766, then French), minus Savoy/Nice (Savoyard). **Includes** Alsace (acquired 1648–81), Franche-Comté.  
**Capital**: Versailles/Paris  
**Key cities**: Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux, Rouen, Strasbourg, Lille  
**Ruler**: Louis XV (1715–1774, age 52 in 1763).  
**Foreign Minister**: César Gabriel de Choiseul, Duke of Praslin (signed Treaty of Paris 10 Feb 1763).  
**Source**: Doyle 1989 [MONOGRAPH]; Wikipedia [WEAK] ministerial dates.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**:  
- **Corsica**: Must be Genoese in 1763; sold to France 1768.  
- **Lorraine**: Autonomous 1763 (Stanisław Leszczyński ex-King of Poland); French 1766.

### SPAIN (Bourbon)
**Territorial extent**: Iberian peninsula minus Portugal; Balearic Islands. Spanish Netherlands LOST to Austria 1714.  
**Capital**: Madrid  
**Key cities**: Barcelona, Seville, Valencia, Cádiz, Zaragoza  
**Ruler**: Charles III (r. 1759–88, age 46 in 1763); initiating Bourbon Reforms.  
**First Secretary of State**: Ricardo Wall (15 May 1754 – 9 Oct 1763; resigned 235 days after Treaty of Paris).  
**Source**: Lynch 1989 [MONOGRAPH]; Wikipedia [WEAK] ministerial dates.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**: None. War of Spanish Succession settled Bourbon legitimacy but cost European possessions.

### PORTUGAL
**Territorial extent**: Modern Portugal, including Algarve.  
**Capital**: Lisbon  
**Key cities**: Porto, Coimbra, Évora  
**Ruler**: José I (1750–77, age 48 in 1763).  
**Chief Minister (de facto)**: Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, **Count of Oeiras** (title 1759; Marquis of Pombal from 1769/1770). Secretary of State for Internal Affairs (1756–1777).  
**Source**: Maxwell 1995 [MONOGRAPH]; Wikipedia [WEAK] title dates.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**: None. Independent since 1640 (end of Iberian Union).

### DUTCH REPUBLIC (United Provinces)
**Territorial extent**: Seven provinces (Holland, Zeeland, Utrecht, Gelderland, Overijssel, Friesland, Groningen) + Generality Lands (Brabant, Limburg, Flanders areas).  
**Capital**: The Hague (seat of government); Amsterdam (largest city).  
**Key cities**: Rotterdam, Utrecht, Leiden, Haarlem, Groningen  
**Stadtholder**: William V, Prince of Orange (acceded 1751 at age 3; age 14–15 in Feb 1763; came of age 1766).  
**Regents (1763)**: Princess Marie Louise of Hesse-Kassel (1759–9 March 1765) + Duke Louis Ernest of Brunswick-Lüneburg (1759–1766 co-regent).  
**Source**: Israel 1995 [MONOGRAPH]; Wikipedia [WEAK] regency structure.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**: None. **CRITICAL**: Austrian Netherlands = Habsburg (modern Belgium), NOT Dutch until 1815 Congress of Vienna.

### AUSTRIAN NETHERLANDS
**Territorial extent**: Modern Belgium and Luxembourg (under Habsburg personal union, NOT part of Dutch Republic).  
**Capital**: Brussels  
**Sovereign**: Maria Theresa, Archduchess of Austria, Queen of Hungary and Bohemia (1740–80, age 45 in 1763).  
**Governor-General**: Prince Charles Alexander of Lorraine (1744 – 4 July 1780); brother-in-law of Maria Theresa; "de facto sovereign" administrator.  
**Source**: Wikipedia [WEAK] Austrian Netherlands governance; Ingrao 2000 [MONOGRAPH] Habsburg territories.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**: **CRITICAL**: Belgium/Luxembourg = AUSTRIAN not Dutch 1763. Incorporated into Kingdom of Netherlands only 1815 (Congress of Vienna).

### SWISS CONFEDERATION
**Territorial extent**: Thirteen cantons (core) + associated territories; roughly modern Switzerland **minus** Geneva (independent city-republic, not yet Swiss) & Neuchâtel (Prussian possession 1707–1848).  
**Capital**: None (confederation); Bern, Zürich, Luzern as major centers.  
**Key cities**: Basel, Geneva (independent), Lausanne  
**Source**: Bonjour et al. 1952 [MONOGRAPH]; neutrality established 1648.  
**ANACHRONISM FLAGS**: Geneva = independent 1763, joins Swiss Confederation 1815. Neuchâtel = Prussian until 1848.

### DENMARK-NORWAY
**Territorial extent**: Denmark proper (Jutland, Funen, Zealand, Bornholm); **Norway** (entire; subordinate since 1536); duchies of Schleswig & Holstein (personal union; Holstein = HRE status).  
**Capital**: Copenhagen  
**Key cities**: Bergen, Christiania (Oslo), Trondheim, Odense, Aarhus

### Denmark-Norway
**Norwegian primary sectors [PRIMARY]**:

1. **Timber and lumber (PRIMARY EXPORT)**: Major industry by 17th century; exports "especially to England" and foreign navies. Geographic centers: southern/eastern Norway river valleys (Glomma, Lægen); shipping ports Oslo (Christiania), Frederikstad, Moss, Sandefjord (east coast), Bergen (west coast). Royal decree 1688 restricted sawmill operations (anti-deforestation); small number of merchants controlled entire industry under Danish royal monopoly [PRIMARY].

2. **Fishing and preserved fish products**: **Stockfish** (dried unsalted cod) = "Norway's longest sustained export commodity" (13th century onward); ~80% water removed via cold-air fermentation, storage life several years; produced Feb–May during cod season; **northern Norway ideal** (Lofoten Islands). Shipped to Mediterranean (Italy as *stoccafisso*), Baltic, England. **Salted cod** (*klippfisk*) emerging 18th century; required salt purchased from Danish merchants (increased Danish control) [PRIMARY].

3. **Mining and metals**: **Kongsberg Silver Mines**, **Røros Copper Mines** (central Norway); operated under Danish royal monopoly charters [PRIMARY].

4. **Shipping and maritime trade**: Norwegian neutral-power advantage 1690–1763 (captured neutral shipping during European wars); supplied foreign navies; many Norwegians worked on foreign (Dutch) vessels. Recovery after Great Northern War (1709–21 disruption) [PRIMARY].

**Danish revenues [PRIMARY]**:  
- **Sound Dues** (Øresundstolden): **Constituted up to 2/3 of Denmark's state income 16th–17th centuries**; still significant 1763. Mechanics: All foreign ships crossing Øresund (Denmark-Sweden strait) halted at **Helsingør** (Elsinore); 1567 shift to **1–2% tax on cargo value** (tripled revenue); Crown right to purchase cargo at declared value (anti-underreporting enforcement); Danish cannons at Helsingør + Swedish cannons at Helsingborg. Traffic: 795 ships (1479) → 2,731 (1560) → 4,262 (1581); substantial by 18th century (all Baltic trade, Mediterranean grain, Northern European commerce) [PRIMARY].

- **Agricultural surplus**: Denmark = subsistence base (80–85% rural); supplied union; noble landowners dominated [PRIMARY].

- **Rents from Norwegian industry**: Up to 2/3 of Norway's audited national income transferred to Copenhagen (colonial-style revenue extraction) [PRIMARY].

- **Colonial trade**: Sugar/spices/slave trade (Tranquebar, West Indies) [PRIMARY].

**Key ports [PRIMARY]**:  
- Denmark: **Copenhagen** (capital, merchant hub, customs/Sound Dues HQ); **Helsingør** (toll collection); Aarhus, Aalborg.  
- Norway: **Oslo** (Christiania, timber export); **Frederikstad** (timber/shipping); Moss; **Bergen** (fish exports, North Sea/Atlantic hub); Stavanger (fishing); Trondheim; Lofoten Islands (cod production).

**Economic complementarity**: Denmark supplied food; Norway supplied timber/metals/fish/shipping. Norwegian industry enjoyed legal monopoly in Denmark. **Norway = industrial/export engine; Denmark = agricultural base + monopoly rent collector** [PRIMARY].

**Source**: 1763_econ_denmark_norway.md [PRIMARY] (census-level inference, trade mechanics); Derry 1979 [MONOGRAPH] Scandinavia.

### Sweden (incl. Finland 1763)
**Iron and copper mining (PRIMARY EXPORT) [MONOGRAPH/PRIMARY]**:

1. **Bergslagen iron mining region**: Central Sweden spanning Värmland, Västmanland, Dalarna, Gävleborg, Örebro provinces (~6,000–8,000 km²). **Production volumes**: ~42,000–46,000 tonnes/year bar-iron exports 1763 (interpolated; 1740s baseline ~40,000 tonnes, 1790s peak ~52,000 tonnes); English imports from Sweden ~20,000 tonnes/year. **Iron constituted ~75% of total Swedish exports by late 18th century** [PRIMARY].

   **Major districts**:  
   - **Värmland** (Filipstad, Sunne, Fryksdalen): Major bar-iron forges; critical export hub.  
   - **Västmanland** (Arboga, Västerås hinterland): Bloomery and forge operations.  
   - **Dalarna** (Falun, Hedemora, Säter): Mixed copper/iron (Kopparbergslagen); bar-iron forges.  
   - **Gävleborg** (Gävle, Ljusdal): Coastal trade exit; bar-iron shipment point.  
   - **Örebro** (Örebro, Lindesberg): Forges and re-export warehouses.

   **Production network**: Bloomeries (*småbruk*) using local bog ore + charcoal → Forges (*bruk*) converting pig iron to bar iron → Export pipeline via Gävle, Västerås, Värmland ports → Gothenburg (west coast) or Stockholm/Västervik (Baltic) → European markets (England, Netherlands, Hamburg) [PRIMARY].

2. **Falun copper mine** (Kopparberg, Dalarna province): Peak production era 17th century; still commercially active 1763 but secondary to 17th-century output. Refined copper bars for European markets; supplied domestic arms, bells, metalware. Part of Kopparbergslagen industrial district (copper+iron combined) [PRIMARY].

**Tar production (*pix suecica*) [PRIMARY]**: Primary source regions: Swedish Norrland boreal forests (Västernorrland, Jämtland, Norrbotten); Ume River valley, Torne River region; upper Dalarna, Gävleborg forest belt. Pine tar extraction via charring in pit kilns; dispersed cottage-industry (*tarverk*); seasonal (spring/early summer). Export: English/Dutch shipyards (naval stores); 5–10% of Swedish exports by value 1763 [PRIMARY].

**Timber/lumber exports [PRIMARY]**: Norrland forests (Västernorrland, Jämtland, Norrbotten) primary long-timber source; upper Svealand (Gävleborg, Dalarna, Västmanland) supplementary. Log-export routes: River drives (Göta Älv, Klarälv, Göte Älv) → west-coast ports. Buyers: English shipyards, Dutch traders, West European construction; 3–8% of export value [PRIMARY].

**Mining regulation**: **Bergskollegium** (Swedish Bureau of Mines, founded 1637): Crown monopoly over all mining; licensing of private forges; export licensing and tax collection; technical standardization (bar-iron quality, weight); travelogue documentation (study missions) [PRIMARY].

**Port infrastructure [PRIMARY]**:  
- **Gothenburg** (west coast): Primary export hub for Värmland iron and timber (Atlantic/North Sea routes).  
- **Gävle** (east coast, Gävleborg): Major iron and timber export point to Baltic.  
- **Stockholm** (central Baltic): Administrative hub; secondary export point for copper, tar, inland-origin goods.  
- **Västervik** (southeast, Östergötland): Secondary export point for tar and timber.

**Source**: 1763_econ_sweden_mining.md [MONOGRAPH/PRIMARY] (Jernkontoret 30-year Atlas project, Uppsala University research, Oslo copper studies, SCB historical statistics 1732–1970, Taylor & Francis 1982 peer-reviewed journal); Roberts 1986 [MONOGRAPH] Age of Liberty.

## 1763-1815 TRANSFORMATION SUMMARY

**THIS IS THE SINGLE LARGEST EUROPEAN MAP CHANGE** between 1763 and 1815 [MONOGRAPH: Wilson].

### A. Napoleonic Dissolution of HRE (1803-1806)

**1803: Reichsdeputationshauptschluss** (Imperial Recess / Final Recess) [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

Context: French Revolutionary Wars (1792-1802) saw France annex left bank of Rhine (Treaty of Lunéville 1801). HRE princes lost Rhine territories; compensation = secularization + mediatization [MONOGRAPH: Wilson].

**SECULARIZATION** (*Säkularisation*) — ALL ecclesiastical territories dissolved:
- **3 ecclesiastical Electorates** (Mainz, Trier, Cologne) abolished [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **ALL prince-bishoprics** secularized (Salzburg, Würzburg, Bamberg, Münster, Paderborn, Hildesheim, Freising, Passau, Augsburg, etc.) absorbed by secular neighbors [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **All prince-abbacies** dissolved [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

**MEDIATIZATION** (*Mediatisierung*) — **45 of 51 imperial free cities** lost independence:
- Absorbed by larger states: Nürnberg, Augsburg, Regensburg, Ulm, Aachen, Cologne, Worms, Speyer, etc. → Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, Prussia [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- Only **4 survived to 1806**: Hamburg, Bremen, Lübeck, Frankfurt (all survived as sovereign cities into 19th century) [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

**Beneficiaries**:
- **Baden** — gained Konstanz, Basel territories; raised to Electorate 1803 [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **Württemberg** — gained bishoprics, abbeys, free cities; raised to Electorate 1803 [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **Bavaria** — gained Würzburg, Bamberg, Freising, Augsburg, Salzburg territories, Swabia; raised to Electorate [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **Prussia** — gained Hildesheim, Paderborn, Münster, Erfurt [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

**Result**: HRE shrank from **~300 states to ~100** by 1803 [MONOGRAPH: Wilson].

**1806: Dissolution of Holy Roman Empire** [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

- **Confederation of the Rhine** (*Rheinbund*) formed 12 July 1806 under French protection: Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, Hesse-Darmstadt, Nassau, Berg, others left HRE [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **Francis II** abdicated as Holy Roman Emperor **6 August 1806** (retaining title Emperor of Austria, adopted 1804) [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **HRE ceased to exist** [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

**1815: German Confederation** [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

Congress of Vienna (1814-1815) created **German Confederation** (*Deutscher Bund*): **39 sovereign states** (vs. ~300 in 1763):
- Austria presided (until 1866) [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **4 Free Cities** remained: Frankfurt, Hamburg, Bremen, Lübeck [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- **NO ecclesiastical states** [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]
- Prussia gained Rhineland, Westphalia [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

### B. Napoleonic Transformation of Italy (1797-1815)

- **Venice**: Extinguished 1797 (Campo Formio) → Austrian **Kingdom of Lombardy-Venetia** 1815 [MONOGRAPH: Lane]
- **Genoa**: Annexed 1805 (Ligurian Republic 1797-1805) → Kingdom of Sardinia 1815 [MONOGRAPH: Costantini]
- **Papal States**: Rome annexed 1809-1814 → restored 1815 minus Avignon [MONOGRAPH: Gross]
- **Naples + Sicily**: Murat interregnum 1808-1815 → Bourbon restored, merged into **Two Sicilies** 1816 [MONOGRAPH: Acton]
- **Tuscany**: French interregnum 1799-1814 → Habsburg-Lorraine restored 1815 [MONOGRAPH: Diaz]
- **Parma**: To Marie Louise (Napoleon's wife) 1815; reverts to Bourbon 1847 [MONOGRAPH: Carpanetto]
- **Lucca**: Made principality (Bourbon-Parma); to Tuscany 1847 [MONOGRAPH: 1763_WORLD_Italy.md]

## SUMMARY

**Central Europe (HRE)**: ~300 polities in 1763 (8 Electors, ~50 prince-bishoprics, ~51 free cities, hundreds of minor states) → 39 German Confederation states by 1815 (all ecclesiastical states dissolved 1803, free cities mostly mediatized) [MONOGRAPH: Wilson]

**Italy**: ~10 independent polities in 1763 (Venice, Genoa, Sardinia, Milan/Austrian, Tuscany, Modena, Parma, Lucca, Papal States, Naples, Sicily) → Venice/Genoa extinguished, Naples+Sicily merged, Austria gains Lombardy-Venetia, Sardinia gains Genoa [MONOGRAPH: Lane, Carpanetto, Acton]

**Economic character**: Pre-industrial 1763 (craft guilds, agriculture-dominant, mercantilist trade, luxury crafts); early industrial stirrings by 1815 (British textile competition, steam power emerging, Continental System disruption 1806-1814) [MONOGRAPH: Ogilvie, Lane]

## OWNERSHIP (political control, Feb 1763)

### Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth (Crown of Poland + Grand Duchy of Lithuania, royal union)

**FULL pre-partition extent** — the Commonwealth stretched across approximately 830,000 km², making it one of Europe's largest states in 1763:

#### Crown Lands (Kingdom of Poland)
- **Greater Poland (Wielkopolska)** — Capital: Poznań. Primary grain-producing region; wheat and rye exports via Vistula/Oder river access to Danzig. West-central location; direct Oder River competitor to Vistula route. [1763_econ_poland_lithuania.md lines 32-36]
- **Lesser Poland (Małopolska)** — Capital: Kraków (coronation city). South-central; includes Galicia (Red Ruthenia, capital Lwów/Lviv). - **Mazovia (Masovia)** — Capital: Warsaw (administrative capital of Commonwealth since 1596). Central Poland north of Vistula; the **most productive grain region** in the Commonwealth. Massive enserfed populations (50-100k serfs per county) on szlachta estates; rye/wheat/barley exports. Warsaw is major Vistula warehouse/transit hub, 300 km from Danzig. [lines 18-24, 113]
- **Podlachia** — Northeast of Mazovia; forms transitional zone between Poland and Lithuania. Grain and forest products. [inferred from 1763_rulers_poland_lithuania.md territorial list]
- **Royal Prussia (Pomerelia)** — Capital: Danzig/Gdańsk (free city under Polish king). Baltic coast; includes Toruń and Elbląg. **Primary export terminus** for Commonwealth grain; 50,000-80,000 tons/year passed through Danzig to Dutch, English, French, Spanish markets. Vistula River mouth; deep-water harbor. [1763_econ_poland_lithuania.md lines 123-143]
- **Galicia / Red Ruthenia** — Capital: Lwów (Lviv). South-central Commonwealth (modern Ukrainian border); Carpathian foothills. Included in Lesser Poland administrative structure. [line 39]
- **Volhynia** — West-central Ukraine; RIGHT-bank (west of Dnieper). Part of Lesser Poland Province. Grain production region. [1763_rulers_poland_lithuania.md line 11]

- **RIGHT-BANK UKRAINE** — West of Dnieper River: Bila Tserkva, Fastiv, Cherkasy, Berdychiv. Part of Lesser Poland Province (Kiev and Bracław voivodeships). [1763_rulers_poland_lithuania.md line 11; audit_worklists/research/europe_1763.md line 58]

### Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth (aggregate ~11-12 million)

**Aggregate figure**: 11-12 million total population in 1763. [Inferred from 1763_econ_poland_lithuania.md serf population percentages and regional descriptions; explicit aggregate not stated, but consistent with historiographic consensus for pre-partition Commonwealth — Stone (2001), Lukowski & Zawadzki (2006)]

#### Rural / Serf Composition
- **Enserfment rate**: 60-75% of rural population in core grain regions (Mazovia, Greater Poland, Podolia, Lesser Poland). [1763_econ_poland_lithuania.md line 94]
- **Serf categories**:
  - *Kmiecie* (peasant-farmers): Hereditary semi-free status; held allotments; owed barshchina (labor obligation) 3-6 days/week on demesne.
  - *Parobcy* (farmhands): Landless; owed unlimited labor; extreme servility.
  - *Dzierżawcy* (leaseholders): Middle category; sometimes free; paid cash rent. [lines 96-99]
- **Labor intensity**: Grain monoculture required peak labor 6-8 weeks (May-Sept harvest); folwarki (demesne estates) supplemented serf labor with hired workers (often Jewish) during harvest. [lines 101-102]

#### Magnate Landownership
- **Magnate class**: 20-40 richest families owned ~15-20% of Commonwealth land and ~40-50% of cultivable demesne. [line 161]
- **Enserfed population under magnates**: 3,000-15,000 serfs per magnate family. Example: Potocki family (Podolia/Ruthenia) controlled 8,000-12,000 tons/year grain output from latifundia >200k hectares (~400k hectares across holdings). [lines 165-179]

#### Szlachta (Nobility)
- **Proportion**: 8-15% of total population (unusually high compared to ~1% in France/Italy). [1763_rulers_poland_lithuania.md line 45]
- **Legal status**: Hereditary nobility; held land allodially; elected monarch; served in Sejm; "Golden Liberty" protections. [lines 46-54]

#### Urban / Merchant
- **Danzig/Gdańsk**: Free city; deep-water harbor. Grain merchant community: Dutch (40-45% of trade), English (15-20%), Hanseatic Germans, Danzig burghers (Polish merchants), Jewish traders (license-holders as middlemen). [1763_econ_poland_lithuania.md lines 130-136]
- **Warsaw**: Administrative capital; wholesale/transit hub for Vistula grain traffic. Magnates maintained urban residences; burgher/Jewish merchant agents. [lines 113, 178]

#### Ethnic / Religious Composition
- **Polish** (Catholic): Dominant in Crown lands (Greater Poland, Mazovia, Lesser Poland).
- **Lithuanian** (Catholic): Dominant in Grand Duchy core (Vilnius, Trakai, Samogitia).
- **Ruthenian / Ukrainian** (Orthodox, Uniate): Right-bank Ukraine (Volhynia, Podolia, Kiev voivodeship); Belarus (Minsk, Polotsk, Vitebsk). Uniate Church (1596 Union of Brest) significant in western Ukraine and Belarus.
- **Jewish**: Urban centers (Warsaw, Kraków, Vilnius, Lwów); merchant/leaseholder/agent roles; rural areas (manor agents, distillery/mill operators, harvest labor). Estimated ~750,000-1 million Jews in Commonwealth by 1764 (largest Jewish population in world). [Inferred from 1763_econ_poland_lithuania.md merchant roles; historiographic consensus — Stone (2001)]
- **German**: Royal Prussia urban centers (Danzig, Toruń, Elbląg); burgher class. Baltic Livonia towns.
- **Latvian / Latgalian**: Latgalia (Daugavpils, Rēzekne region); Grand Duchy territories.

### Ottoman autonomous vassals / tributaries

#### Crimean Khanate

**Khan**: Qırım Giray (قرم كراى, first reign 1758–1764; in power throughout 1763)  
**Dynasty**: Giray dynasty (exclusively appointed by Sultan from this family after 1584)  
**Capital**: Bakhchisaray  
**Status**: **Privileged vassal** (Kolodziejczyk: nuanced between vassalage and independence)

**Unusual vassal model**:
- **NO regular tribute paid to Ottomans** (reverse flow: Ottomans paid subsidies and gifts to Khanate)
- **Military service exchange**: Primary obligation was providing tens of thousands of light cavalry for Ottoman campaigns (considered "indispensable" / *vazgeçilemez*)
- **High internal autonomy**: Full control over domestic administration, justice, taxation, religious affairs (Khan held title "Caliph of the North")
- **Constrained foreign policy**: Especially with Russia required Ottoman coordination; Ottoman military presence in coastal fortresses (e.g., Caffa/Kefe)
- **Appointment**: Sultan had full appointment and removal authority by 1763, selecting exclusively from Giray dynasty

**CRITICAL DELTA**: Crimean Khanate **EXISTS in 1763**, **ABSENT in 1815** (annexed by Russia 1783)

#### Danubian Principalities (Moldavia & Wallachia)

**Phanariote System** (inaugurated 1711 Moldavia, 1715 Wallachia): After local elective systems abandoned, Ottomans appointed **Phanariote Greeks** from Constantinople through bribery-based system.

**Moldavia**:
- **Hospodar**: Grigore Callimachi (Γρηγόριος Καλλιμάχης, first reign 1761–1764; in power throughout 1763)
- **Status**: Ottoman vassal with high internal autonomy
- **Appointment**: Candidates typically served as chief dragoman of the Porte, purchased office via generous bribe
- **Tribute**: Paid directly to Porte; levels "directly proportional with Ottoman requests" (declined significantly after 1774 Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca to ~3,100 gold coins by 1820s)
- **Autonomy**: Retained internal self-government (own princes, courts, boyar councils), full control over domestic administration/justice, autonomous in religious affairs (Orthodox Christian)
- **Constraints**: Princely appointments entirely dependent on Sultan; no independent foreign policy; required to participate in Ottoman military campaigns when requested; **Russian intervention rights** established after 1774 Treaty

**Wallachia**:
- **Hospodar**: Constantin Mavrocordat (Κωνσταντίνος Μαυροκορδάτος, reign 1761–1763; in power during February 1763, reign ending that year)
- **Status**: Ottoman vassal with high internal autonomy
- **Appointment**: Same bribery-based system as Moldavia; **Wallachia commanded higher bribes** than Moldavia (wealthier province)
- **Tribute**: Paid to Porte; dropped from ~50,000 to ~20,000 gold coins by 1820s after 1774 Treaty
- **Autonomy**: Same high degree of internal autonomy as Moldavia
- **Constraints**: Same as Moldavia

**1763 vs 1815 Delta**: Vassal status continues both dates, but **Russian influence increases** after 1774 Treaty of Küçük Kaynarca, creating dual Ottoman-Russian pressure by 1815.

#### Persia (Zand Dynasty)

**Ruler**: Karim Khan Zand (کریم خان زند, c. 1705–1779)  
**Title**: *Vakil ol-Ra'aya* (وکیل الرعایا, "Deputy of the People" / "Representative of the People") — **notably refused** the title "Shah," adopting a humbler designation  
**Capital**: **Shiraz** (شیراز) — NOT Tehran  
**Dynasty**: Zand dynasty (زندیان), founded 1751  
**Religion**: Twelver Shia Islam  
**Origins**: From the Zand tribe of the Laks (Kurdish/Lur ethnicity)

**Territorial extent (1763)**:
- **Core Persia**: Nearly all of Iran except Khorasan and Balochistan
- **Exclusions**: 
  - **Khorasan**: Held by Shahrokh Shah (Afsharid remnant) in Mashhad as SEPARATE polity
  - **Balochistan**: Not under Zand control
- Did not subdue Khorasan throughout his reign

**Governance style**:
- Deliberately small bureaucracy
- **Personal rule**: Karim Khan personally handled state affairs
- Provincial administration followed Safavid model (*beglerbegis*, *kalantar*, *darugha*, *kadkhuda*)
- Tribal administration created new posts (*ilkhani* for Lur tribes, *ilbegi* for Qashqai tribes)
- **Religious policy**: Unlike Safavids, did not seek *ulama* (clerical) approval for authority

**Character**: Contemporary and historical sources praise Karim Khan for generosity, modesty, and fairness. The Oxford Dictionary of Islam calls him "**the most humane Iranian ruler of the Islamic era**." Wore simple clothing, sat on inexpensive carpet rather than throne.

**Condition of Persia under Karim Khan**: Iran recovered from roughly 40 years of devastation following Nader Shah's death (1747); gained renewed "tranquillity, security, peace, and prosperity"; **peak of Zand rule 1765–1779**.

**1763 Status**: By 1763, Karim Khan had been ruling for 12 years and had consolidated his power. That year (1763), he brought **Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar** and Hosayn Qoli Khan to his capital — the Qajar hostage who would later overthrow the Zand dynasty.

**Economy** (Shiraz-centered):
- **Shiraz**: Political and commercial center; population 40,000–50,000 by late 18th century (surpassing Isfahan)
- **Trade hub**: Attracted foreign traders; British East India Company permitted southern trading post
- **Carpets**: Shiraz, Isfahan, Kerman, Tabriz (Persian carpets world-renowned)
- **Silk**: Gilan province (primary silk-producing region; major export to Russia via Caspian)
- **Dried fruits**: Dates, figs, apricots, raisins, pistachios
- **Persian Gulf port**: Bushehr (primary Gulf port; trade with Oman, India, East Africa)

**Afsharid Khorasan (SEPARATE POLITY in 1763)**:
- **Ruler**: Shahrokh Shah (شاهرخ شاه, 1734–1796; second reign 1750–1796; in power throughout 1763)
- **Capital**: Mashhad (مشهد)
- **Territory**: Western Khorasan (roughly modern Khorasan Province)
- **Status**: Largely a figurehead; real power held by Kurdish, Arab, and Turkic tribal chieftains; effectively a **Durrani client** (Ahmad Shah Durrani invaded multiple times; restored Shahrokh as king 9 May 1755)
- **Legitimacy claim**: Half-Safavid descent (mother was Safavid princess)
- **Death**: 1796, when Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar conquered Khorasan and tortured him to extract Nader's hidden treasures

**MAJOR CHANGE — 1763 vs 1815**:
- **1763**: **Zand dynasty**, capital **Shiraz**, ruled by Karim Khan Zand
- **1815**: **Qajar dynasty**, capital **Tehran**, ruled by Fath-Ali Shah Qajar (r. 1797–1834)
- **Different ruling family, different capital, different governance style**
- Zand dynasty completely destroyed 1794 by Agha Mohammad Khan Qajar

Source: [MONOGRAPH] 1763_WORLD_Ottoman_MENA.md lines 880-1109; [PRIMARY] asia_middleeast_1763.md line 9; [PRIMARY] 1763_DELTA_Ottoman_MENA.md lines 115-164; Wikipedia: Karim Khan Zand; Wikipedia: Zand dynasty; Wikipedia: Shahrokh Shah; The Oxford Dictionary of Islam

## OWNERSHIP (political control, Feb 1763)

### European coastal footholds (SMALL — not territorial empires)

**Critical framing**: In 1763, European presence in Sub-Saharan Africa is confined to coastal forts, the Cape Colony (still small), and thin trading settlements. The vast interior is controlled by independent African states or is stateless. This changes only after the 1880s Scramble for Africa.

- **Portuguese**: 
  - **Angola**: Luanda (capital, São Paulo de Luanda), Benguela, Massangano; coastal control + interior *prazeros* (warlord-merchant estates); slave-trade driven (Luanda exports ~10,000–15,000 slaves/year to Brazil). Interior penetration limited; relies on African intermediaries (Lunda, Luba, Ovimbundu). [MONOGRAPH] Miller (1988) *Way of Death*.
  - **Mozambique**: Mozambique Island (Ilha de Moçambique, capital), Sofala, Inhambane, Sena, Tete (Zambezi valley *prazos*); weak inland authority; prazos are semi-autonomous Afro-Portuguese fiefdoms. [MONOGRAPH] Newitt (1995).
  - **Cape Verde, Guinea forts** (Bissau area): Minor coastal footholds.
  
- **Dutch VOC**: 
  - **Cape Colony**: Cape Town (Kaapstad, capital), Stellenbosch, Swellendam, Paarl. Governor Ryk Tulbagh (r. 1751–1771). Small area (~100–150 km radius from Cape Town); expanding inland via trekboer (Boer pastoralist) frontier. NOT the British Cape of 1815 (Britain seizes 1806, formalizes 1814). [MONOGRAPH] Giliomee (2003).
  - **Gold Coast forts**: Elmina (since 1637), scattered trading posts.

- **British**: 
  - **Gold Coast**: Cape Coast Castle (headquarters), Anomabo, other forts. Competition for gold and slaves.
  - **Senegambia**: Fort James (Gambia River, ceded to Britain 1763 at Treaty of Paris).

- **French**: 
  - **Senegambia**: Saint-Louis (Senegal River), Gorée Island (slave depot). [MONOGRAPH] Barry (1998).
  - **Slave Coast** (Bight of Benin): Factories at Porto-Novo, Ouidah area.

- **Danish**: 
  - **Gold Coast**: Christiansborg/Osu (Accra area).

**Extent**: These are **POINTS**, not territorial control. The interior is independent African states or stateless zones. European holdings = coastal strips, forts, and Cape Colony (small).

### Independent African states (extent + status)

#### **WEST AFRICA**

1. **Asante Empire / Ashanti** (Asanteman, Twi)
   - **Capital**: Kumasi
   - **Extent**: Central Ghana forest zone; controlled Akan goldfields; expanding toward coast (Fante wars ongoing).
   - **Status**: STRONG — hierarchical confederacy under Asantehene (Osei Kwadwo succeeds 1764, regent Kusi Obodom d. 1764) with Golden Stool ideology, standing army, tribute system. Founded c. 1701 under Osei Tutu; by 1763 dominant military power in Gold Coast interior.
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] McCaskie (1995) *State and Society in Pre-Colonial Asante*.

2. **Oyo Empire** (Ilú Ọ̀yọ́, Yoruba)
   - **Capital**: Oyo-Ile (Old Oyo)
   - **Extent**: Southwest Nigeria (Yorubaland); tributary control over Dahomey (1730–1823), Egba/Egbado corridor to coast.
   - **Status**: STRONG — at territorial zenith. Imperial structure with Alaafin (divine king, Alaafin Abiodun r. c. 1754–1789), Oyo Mesi council, cavalry-based military. Law (1977) notes maximum extent by 1760s.
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Law (1977) *The Oyo Empire c.1600–c.1836*.

3. **Kingdom of Dahomey** (Danhomè, Fon)
   - **Capital**: Abomey
   - **Extent**: Southern Benin (Volta–Ouémé rivers); tributary to Oyo from 1730 (annual tribute paid after Oyo conquest 1730).
   - **Status**: STRONG — centralized absolute monarchy under King Tegbesu (r. 1740–1774); standing army including female soldiers (Mino/Amazons); slave-trade monopoly (Ouidah/Whydah port, conquered 1727).
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Bay (1998) *Wives of the Leopard*.

4. **Kingdom of Benin** (Ẹdo, Edo language)
   - **Capital**: Benin City (Ẹdo)
   - **Extent**: South-central Nigeria (Edo State region); reduced from 16th-17th century peak but still significant.
   - **Status**: STRONG — divine kingship (Oba) with titled palace chiefs, guilds, tributary system. Declined from apogee but centralized royal authority maintained.
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Ryder (1969) *Benin and the Europeans 1485–1897*.

5. **Hausa City-States** (Kasashen Hausa, Hausa) — DECENTRALIZED
   - **Major states**: Kano (capital: Kano, major commercial/manufacturing center); Katsina (capital: Katsina, Saharan trade terminus); Zazzau/Zaria (capital: Zaria, slave-raiding state); Gobir (capital: Alkalawa, northern defensive frontier); Kebbi (capital: Birnin Kebbi); Daura, Rano, Biram.
   - **Extent**: Northern Nigeria, southern Niger; each city-state controlled surrounding hinterland.
   - **Status**: DECENTRALIZED — seven (or more) independent walled city-states, no unified authority. Each had centralized urban government (Sarki/king) but were rivals, frequently warring. **No Sokoto Caliphate in 1763** (founded 1804 via Fulani jihad under Usman dan Fodio).
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Smith (1997) *Government in Kano 1350–1950*; Last (1967) *The Sokoto Caliphate* (emphasizes pre-1804 fragmentation).

6. **Kanem-Bornu Empire** (Sultanate of Bornu, Kanuri)
   - **Capital**: Ngazargamu (until 1808)
   - **Extent**: Northeast Nigeria, southern Chad, northern Cameroon; controlled Saharan trade routes to Tripoli.
   - **Status**: STRONG — Sayfawa dynasty sultanate under Mai Dunama IX (r. 1747–1792); feudal provincial structure, Islamic administration, tributary network.
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Brenner (1973) *The Shehus of Kukawa*.

7. **Bamana Empire of Segu** (Segu fanga, Bambara/Bamana)
   - **Capital**: Ségou (on Niger River)
   - **Extent**: Central Mali along middle Niger River; controlled Niger riverine trade between Timbuktu and upper Niger.
   - **Status**: STRONG — military kingdom with *ton-dyon* (slave army); transition period (Biton Coulibaly dynasty ending; Ngolo Diarra founds new line 1766, just after 1763).
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Roberts (1987) *Warriors, Merchants, and Slaves*.

8. **Kingdom of Kong** (Kong Empire, Dyula/Juula)
   - **Capital**: Kong (northern Ivory Coast)
   - **Extent**: Northern Ivory Coast, southern Burkina Faso; commercial empire linking forest and savanna trade.
   - **Status**: STRONG — Dyula merchant-warrior state, Islamic, military expansion under Sekou Ouattara dynasty (founded 1710). At expansionist peak mid-18th century.
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Wilks (1982) "Wangara, Akan and Portuguese" in *Journal of African History*.

9. **Futa Jallon** (Fuuta Jaloo, Pular/Fulfulde)
   - **Capital**: Timbo (alternating with Fugumba)
   - **Extent**: Guinea highlands (Fouta Djallon massif).
   - **Status**: STRONG — theocratic imamate, confederal structure with alternating Almamys (from Soriya and Alphaya clans); founded c. 1725–1750s after Fulani jihad against Jallonke/Susu rulers. By 1763 consolidating under dual Almamy rotating system.
   - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Barry (1998) *Senegambia and the Atlantic Slave Trade*.

10. **Mossi Kingdoms** (Moogo, Moore) — DECENTRALIZED
    - **Major kingdoms**: Ouagadougou (Wogodogo, most powerful); Yatenga (capital: Ouahigouya); Tenkodogo (ancestral center); Fada N'gourma.
    - **Extent**: Central Burkina Faso.
    - **Status**: DECENTRALIZED — multiple independent kingdoms sharing Mossi culture; each with centralized monarchy (Mogho Naba) but politically separate, often rivals. Successfully resisted Songhai and later jihadist conquest.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Izard (1985) *Gens du pouvoir, gens de la terre*.

11. **Songhai successor states** — FRAGMENTED
    - **Context**: After Moroccan conquest (1591), Songhai fragmented. By 1763:
      - **Dendi kingdom** (small rump state on Niger, capital Lulami) — minor.
      - **Arma rulers of Timbuktu** (Moroccan military caste, much degraded by 1763) — weak.
      - Tuareg confederations controlling desert.
    - **Status**: NO effective centralized authority in former Songhai territory; Timbuktu under weak Arma/Tuareg factions.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Kaba (1981) "Archers, Musketeers and Mosquitoes" in *Journal of African History*.

12. **Tuareg Confederations** (Kel Adagh, Kel Ayr, etc.)
    - **Extent**: Saharan Mali, northern Niger, southern Algeria.
    - **Status**: STATELESS/DECENTRALIZED — segmentary lineage society organized in *drums* (Ettebel) under *amenukals* (chiefs); loose confederal structures; pastoralist with some oasis control. No permanent centralized state.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Norris (1975) *The Tuaregs: Their Islamic Legacy*.

#### **CENTRAL AFRICA**

13. **Kingdom of Kongo** (Wene wa Kongo, Kikongo)
    - **Capital**: São Salvador (Mbanza Kongo) — contested/ruined after civil wars.
    - **Extent**: Northern Angola, western DRC, Congo-Brazzaville; GREATLY reduced from 16th-century extent. Soyo effectively independent by 1763.
    - **Status**: FRAGMENTED — after 1665 Battle of Mbwila and ensuing civil wars, Kongo fractured. By 1763 Manikongo (king, possibly Pedro V r. 1763–1764 or late Sebastião I d. 1764) had minimal authority outside capital region; Soyo an independent duchy.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Thornton (1983) *The Kingdom of Kongo: Civil War and Transition, 1641–1718*.

14. **Kingdom of Loango** (Lwããngu, Vili)
    - **Capital**: Loango (Buali)
    - **Extent**: Coastal Congo-Brazzaville and Cabinda enclave; major slave-trading port.
    - **Status**: STRONG — centralized monarchy with Ma-Loango (king); commercial state focused on Atlantic trade. Independent from Kongo since 16th century.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Martin (1972) *The External Trade of the Loango Coast*.

15. **Lunda Empire** (Mwant Yaav, Lunda)
    - **Capital**: Musumba (Katanga region, southeastern DRC)
    - **Extent**: Southeastern DRC (Katanga), northern Zambia, eastern Angola; vast empire through tributary system.
    - **Status**: STRONG — expansive empire under Mwant Yaav (title of emperor; Muteba in 1760s at maximum extent); feudal tributary network with positional succession system (*perpetual kinship*); integration through marriage alliances.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Miller (1976) *Kings and Kinsmen*; Vansina (1966) *Kingdoms of the Savanna*.

16. **Luba Empire** (Luba/Baluba)
    - **Capital**: Mwibele (central Katanga), capitals moved with rulers.
    - **Extent**: Central-southern DRC (Katanga/Shaba region).
    - **Status**: STRONG — sacred kingship (Mulopwe, under Kadilo in 1763) with spiritual authority; *bambudye* secret society providing political cohesion; tributary network. Luba provided political model for many Central African states including Lunda.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Reefe (1981) *The Rainbow and the Kings*.

17. **Kuba Kingdom** (Bushong/Kuba, Bushoong)
    - **Capital**: Nsheng (Mushenge)
    - **Extent**: South-central DRC (Kasai region), between Sankuru and Kasai rivers.
    - **Status**: STRONG — centralized under *nyim* (divine king; Kot aMbweeky I r. 1710–1785); elaborate court culture, artistic/textile production, age-grade system.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Vansina (1978) *The Children of Woot*.

18. **Kazembe Kingdom** (Mwata Kazembe, Lunda offshoot)
    - **Capital**: Mwansabombwe (Luapula valley, northern Zambia/southeastern DRC)
    - **Extent**: Luapula valley; controlled copper and ivory trade to East African coast.
    - **Status**: STRONG — Lunda offshoot founded c. 1740; by 1763 under Kazembe III Lukwesa Ilunga, functionally independent but acknowledging Mwant Yaav; lucrative trade to Kilwa.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Cunnison (1959) *The Luapula Peoples of Northern Rhodesia*.

19. **Matamba Kingdom** (Matamba, Kimbundu)
    - **Capital**: Santa Maria de Matamba
    - **Extent**: Eastern Angola, between Kwango and Cuanza rivers.
    - **Status**: WEAK by 1763 — once powerful under Queen Nzinga (d. 1663), by mid-18th century declining under Portuguese pressure. Still nominally independent but contracting.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Miller (1976) *Kings and Kinsmen*.

20. **Rozvi Empire / Mutapa State** (Rozvi, Shona)
    - **Capital**: Danangombe (Zimbabwe plateau; Great Zimbabwe long abandoned)
    - **Extent**: Zimbabwe plateau, southern Mozambique inland.
    - **Status**: DECLINING — Rozvi Changamire dynasty ruled confederacy of Shona chiefdoms after conquering Mutapa c. 1684; by 1763 declining from 17th-century peak; loose tributary system weakening.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Beach (1980) *The Shona and Zimbabwe, 900–1850*.

#### **EAST AFRICA / HORN**

21. **Ethiopian Empire** (Ityop'iya/Abyssinia, Mängəstä Ityop'ya, Amharic/Ge'ez)
    - **Capital**: Gondar (Gondarine period capital, 1636–1855)
    - **Extent**: Ethiopian highlands (modern Ethiopia except Somali-inhabited lowlands); nominal suzerainty over parts of Eritrea.
    - **Status**: DECLINING — Gondarine period showing fractures; Emperor Iyoas I (r. 1755–1769, son of Empress Mentewab who served as regent) nominally ruled but provincial *rases* (princes) increasingly autonomous. Beginning of "Era of Princes" (*Zemene Mesafint*, 1769–1855) looms after Iyoas's assassination (1769), but in 1763 still nominally centralized.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Crummey (2000) *Land and Society in the Christian Kingdom of Ethiopia*; Marcus (2002) *A History of Ethiopia*.

22. **Funj Sultanate of Sennar** (Saltanat al-Funj, Arabic)
    - **Capital**: Sennar (on Blue Nile)
    - **Extent**: Central Sudan (Blue Nile region, between Ethiopian highlands and White Nile).
    - **Status**: DECLINING — Islamic sultanate, but by 1763 power shifting from Sultan to Hamaj regents (who seized effective power 1761/62). State structures remained but in transition.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Spaulding (1985) *The Heroic Age in Sinnar*.

23. **Sultanate of Darfur** (Dār Fūr, Arabic: "Land of the Fur")
    - **Capital**: El Fasher (al-Fashir)
    - **Extent**: Western Sudan (Darfur region); controlled Saharan trade routes.
    - **Status**: STRONG — centralized Islamic sultanate under Keira dynasty; Sultan Muhammad Tayrab (r. 1752–1785) at peak, defeated Funj, controlled trade to Egypt.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] O'Fahey (1980) *State and Society in Dar Fur*.

24. **Swahili Coast City-States** (under Omani suzerainty) — DECENTRALIZED
    - **Major cities**: Kilwa (Kilwa Kisiwani, declining from medieval peak); Zanzibar (rising under Omani Busaidi control, becomes capital 1832); Mombasa (Fort Jesus, Omani-controlled since 1698; Mazrui family governors 1746–1837, semi-autonomous); Pate (Pate Island, independent sultanate); Lamu (city-state); Malindi, Bagamoyo.
    - **Extent**: East African coast from southern Somalia to northern Mozambique.
    - **Status**: DECENTRALIZED — individual city-states, most under loose Omani (Busaidi dynasty, Imam Ahmad bin Said Al Busaidi r. 1749–1783) suzerainty after Portuguese expulsion (1698–1720s); Pate and others maintained independence or nominal autonomy. Omani capital is **Muscat** (NOT Zanzibar until 1832).
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Sheriff (1987) *Slaves, Spices and Ivory in Zanzibar*.

25. **Kingdom of Buganda** (Buganda, Luganda)
    - **Capital**: Rubaga (Kampala area; capitals rotated, court at Mengo, Nabulagala)
    - **Extent**: North shore of Lake Victoria, modern central Uganda.
    - **Status**: STRONG — highly centralized under Kabaka (king; Kyabaggu r. c. 1750–1780); hierarchical chieftaincy, standing army, expansionist.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Kiwanuka (1972) *A History of Buganda*; Ray (1991) *Myth, Ritual and Kingship in Buganda*.

26. **Kingdom of Bunyoro** (Bunyoro-Kitara, Runyoro)
    - **Capital**: Masindi region (capitals moved)
    - **Extent**: Western Uganda, east of Lake Albert; former dominant power in Great Lakes, declining relative to Buganda by 1763.
    - **Status**: STRONG but contracting — Babiito dynasty, centralized under Omukama (king); losing territory to Buganda expansion.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Uzoigwe (1973) "Pre-Colonial Markets in Bunyoro-Kitara" in *Comparative Studies in Society and History*.

27. **Kingdom of Rwanda** (Rwanda, Kinyarwanda)
    - **Capital**: Royal court itinerant (later fixed at Nyanza)
    - **Extent**: Modern Rwanda, beginning expansion under Mwami Cyilima II Rujugira (r. c. 1756–1765+).
    - **Status**: STRONG — highly centralized under Mwami (king); hierarchical *ubuhake* cattle clientage system, ritual kingship, standing army; expansion underway; mid-18th century as consolidation period.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Vansina (2004) *Antecedents to Modern Rwanda*.

28. **Kingdom of Burundi** (Burundi, Kirundi)
    - **Capital**: Royal court itinerant (Muramvya region)
    - **Extent**: Modern Burundi.
    - **Status**: STRONG — centralized under Mwami (king); *ganwa* princely class, cattle-based hierarchy similar to Rwanda.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Trouwborst (1962) "Le Burundi" in *Les anciens royaumes de la zone interlacustre méridionale*.

#### **SOUTHERN AFRICA**

29. **Dutch Cape Colony** (Kaapkolonie, VOC)
    - **Capital**: Cape Town (Kaapstad)
    - **Extent**: Small area around Cape Town (~100–150 km radius), expanding inland via trekboer (Boer) frontier; by 1763 borders still fluid.
    - **Status**: COLONIAL ADMINISTRATION — VOC (Dutch East India Company) company rule, Governor Ryk Tulbagh (r. 1751–1771) appointed from Batavia/Amsterdam; centralized but small settler population. **Not British** (Britain seizes 1806, formalizes 1814).
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Giliomee (2003) *The Afrikaners: Biography of a People*.

30. **Merina Kingdom** (Madagascar) (Fanjakan'Imerina, Malagasy)
    - **Capital**: Ambohimanga (capital until 1790s; Antananarivo captured 1794)
    - **Extent**: Central highlands of Madagascar; in 1763 still consolidating, small highland kingdom. Major expansion only post-1787 under Andrianampoinimerina.
    - **Status**: EMERGING STRONG — By 1763 under Andrianjafy (r. 1770–1787, approximate), Merina was one of several competing highland kingdoms. Imerina itself divided until 1794; consolidation and imperial expansion came later.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Campbell (2005) *An Economic History of Imperial Madagascar, 1750–1895*.

31. **Sakalava Kingdoms** (Madagascar) (Sakalava, Malagasy) — DECENTRALIZED
    - **Major kingdoms**: Menabe (southern west coast, capital Morondava area); Boina (northern west coast, capital Majunga/Mahajanga).
    - **Extent**: Western Madagascar coast.
    - **Status**: DECENTRALIZED — two major kingdoms, independent of each other, each with centralized monarchy but no unity; slave-raiding and cattle-based economies; rivals not confederates.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Vérin (1986) *The History of Civilisation in North Madagascar*.

32. **Betsimisaraka Confederation** (Madagascar) (Betsimisaraka, Malagasy: "the many inseparable")
    - **Capital**: Fenerive (Fenoarivo Atsinanana)
    - **Extent**: Eastern Madagascar coast.
    - **Status**: DECENTRALIZED — loose confederation under Ratsimilaho (d. 1751) and successors; fragmented after his death but nominal unity maintained.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Vérin (1986).

33. **Xhosa Chiefdoms** (AmaXhosa, Xhosa) — DECENTRALIZED
    - **Major chiefdoms**: Gcaleka, Rharhabe, Ndlambe (subdivisions emerging from 1770s conflicts).
    - **Extent**: Eastern Cape, South Africa; in 1763 Xhosa were expanding southwest, just beginning contact with expanding Cape Colony (First Frontier War 1779, not yet 1763).
    - **Status**: DECENTRALIZED — multiple independent chiefdoms with common culture; paramount chiefs had limited authority over subordinate chiefs. Major split into Gcaleka and Rharhabe occurred mid-18th century.
    - **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Peires (1981) *The House of Phalo*.

### Stateless / unconquered zones

- **Nilotic pastoralist peoples** (South Sudan, northern Uganda, northern Kenya, northern Tanzania): Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, Maasai, Turkana, Samburu. Segmentary lineage societies, age-set systems, no permanent centralized authority (exception: Shilluk *reth*/king had ritual authority but limited coercive power). [MONOGRAPH] Johnson (1994) *Nuer Prophets*. **Note**: Maasai in 1763 still migrating south through Rift Valley, not yet in full southern Kenya/Tanzania range.

- **Zulu / Nguni chiefdoms** (KwaZulu-Natal region, South Africa): Hundreds of small chiefdoms including Zulu clan (one minor chiefdom among many), Mthethwa, Ndwandwe, Qwabe. **No Zulu kingdom in 1763** (founded c. 1816 by Shaka after *Mfecane*). Small-scale chiefdoms of a few thousand people each. [MONOGRAPH] Hamilton (1998) *The Mfecane Aftermath*.

- **Sotho-Tswana peoples** (interior highveld/plateau, Free State, Lesotho, Botswana): Basotho, Batswana and many sub-groups (Kwena, Ngwato, Kgatla, Hurutshe, Rolong). Dispersed agricultural villages and small chiefdoms, no large-scale political authority. Kingdom of Lesotho founded only 1820s under Moshoeshoe I. [MONOGRAPH] Legassick (1969) "The Sotho-Tswana Peoples before 1800" in *African Societies in Southern Africa*.

- **Khoikhoi peoples** (western South Africa, Namibia): Khoikhoi (pastoralists) vs San (hunter-gatherers) — both "Khoisan" language family. Cape Khoikhoi largely dispossessed by Dutch expansion by 1763, reduced to labor or fled inland; Nama (further north) still independent. Pastoralist bands, no centralized authority; decimated by disease and Dutch expansion in Cape. [MONOGRAPH] Elphick (1985) *Khoikhoi and the Founding of White South Africa*.

- **San peoples** (scattered across southern Africa: Kalahari, Namibia, Botswana, South Africa): Many groups (!Kung, Ju/'hoansi, G/wi). Hunter-gatherer bands, egalitarian, no political centralization. [MONOGRAPH] Lee (1979) *The !Kung San*.

- **Tuareg confederations** (Sahara): see above under West Africa.

- **Vast interior**: Hunter-gatherer & segmentary societies across much of the Congo Basin, southern Sudan, southern Angola, interior Tanzania, interior Mozambique.

## POPULATION (anchors; mod scale 4000 people = 1 unit)

### **West Africa (Guinea Coast to Sahel)**

- **Magnitude**: Asante Empire ~1–2 million; Oyo Empire ~1.5–2.5 million (including tributaries); Dahomey ~200,000–300,000; Benin ~100,000–200,000; Hausa city-states (aggregate) ~2–3 million (Kano alone ~50,000–100,000 urban, larger hinterland); Kanem-Bornu ~500,000–1 million; Bamana Segu ~300,000–500,000; Futa Jallon ~500,000; Mossi kingdoms (aggregate) ~500,000–1 million.
- **Dominant cultures/ethnic groups**: Akan (Asante); Yoruba (Oyo, Ife); Fon (Dahomey); Edo (Benin); Hausa (Hausa city-states); Kanuri (Bornu); Bamana/Bambara (Segu); Fula/Fulani (Futa Jallon, pastoral groups); Mossi (Ouagadougou, Yatenga); Tuareg (Sahara); Dyula/Manding (Kong, commercial diaspora).
- **Religion**: Islam dominant in Sahel (Hausa states, Bornu, Segu, Futa Jallon [theocratic], Kong, Tuareg). Traditional/animist in forest zone (Akan, Yoruba [Orisha], Fon [Dahoman], Edo). Christian presence minimal (coastal forts only).
- **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Lovejoy (2012) *Transformations in Slavery* (Hausa); McCaskie (1995), Law (1977), Bay (1998), Smith (1997), Brenner (1973), Roberts (1987), Barry (1998), Izard (1985).

### **Central Africa (Congo Basin to Great Lakes)**

- **Magnitude**: Kongo (fragmented) ~500,000–1 million; Loango ~100,000–200,000; Lunda Empire ~500,000–1 million (vast but low density); Luba Empire ~300,000–500,000; Kuba ~100,000–150,000; Kazembe ~50,000–100,000; Matamba (declining) ~50,000–100,000.
- **Dominant cultures/ethnic groups**: Kikongo (Kongo); Vili (Loango); Lunda, Luba, Kuba (Central Africa Bantu groups); Kambunda (Chokwe); Kimbundu (Matamba).
- **Religion**: Central Africa = syncretic Christian (Kongo, Matamba — Catholic missions since 16th century, but heavily syncretized with traditional); traditional/animist (Lunda, Luba, Kuba — Bantu religion); ancestor worship dominant.
- **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Thornton (1983, 2020), Martin (1972), Vansina (1966, 1978), Reefe (1981), Cunnison (1959), Miller (1976, 1988).

### **East Africa / Horn**

- **Magnitude**: Ethiopian Empire ~4–6 million (largest in region); Funj Sultanate ~500,000–1 million; Darfur ~200,000–500,000; Swahili coast city-states (aggregate) ~200,000–300,000; Buganda ~500,000–1 million; Bunyoro ~300,000–500,000; Rwanda ~500,000–1 million; Burundi ~300,000–500,000. Nilotic pastoralists (Dinka, Nuer, Maasai, etc., aggregate) ~1–2 million.
- **Dominant cultures/ethnic groups**: Amhara, Tigray, Oromo, Sidama (Ethiopia); Funj (Sennar); Fur (Darfur); Swahili (coastal, Bantu + Arab mixture); Omani Arabs (Zanzibar, Mombasa); Ganda (Buganda); Nyoro (Bunyoro); Rwanda, Rundi (Rwanda, Burundi); Dinka, Nuer, Shilluk, Maasai (Nilotic pastoralists).
- **Religion**: **Christianity** (Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church dominant in Ethiopian highlands — Coptic tradition). **Islam** in Swahili coast (Shafi'i Sunni), Omani Zanzibar/Mombasa (Ibadi), Funj Sennar (Sunni), Darfur (Sunni), some Oromo/Somali lowlands. Traditional/animist in Great Lakes kingdoms (Buganda, Bunyoro, Rwanda, Burundi — Bantu religion) and Nilotic pastoralists.
- **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Crummey (2000), Marcus (2002), Spaulding (1985), O'Fahey (1980), Sheriff (1987), Kiwanuka (1972), Ray (1991), Vansina (2004), Trouwborst (1962), Johnson (1994).

### **Southern Africa**

- **Magnitude**: Cape Colony (VOC) ~20,000–25,000 (settlers + slaves); Rozvi Empire (declining) ~100,000–200,000; Merina (pre-expansion) ~100,000–200,000; Sakalava kingdoms (aggregate) ~100,000–200,000; Betsimisaraka ~50,000–100,000; Xhosa chiefdoms ~100,000–200,000; Nguni chiefdoms (aggregate, pre-Zulu) ~500,000–1 million; Sotho-Tswana (aggregate) ~500,000–1 million; Khoikhoi (remnant) ~50,000–100,000; San ~50,000–100,000.
- **Dominant cultures/ethnic groups**: Dutch settlers + Khoikhoi/San (Cape); Shona (Rozvi); Merina, Sakalava, Betsimisaraka (Madagascar, Malagasy); Xhosa, Nguni (incl. Zulu clan), Sotho, Tswana (Bantu-speaking southern Africa); Khoikhoi, San (Khoisan language family).
- **Religion**: Dutch Reformed (Cape Colony settlers); traditional/animist (Shona ancestor worship, Rozvi; Malagasy traditional; Nguni/Xhosa/Sotho-Tswana traditional). Some syncretic Christian in Madagascar coastal areas (Betsimisaraka, French/Portuguese contact).

## ECONOMIC CONTEXT (1763)

### Atlantic Slave Trade (Peak Period)

- **Volume**: ~80,000 slaves/year embarked from Africa in 1760s (18th-century zenith).
- **Major regions**: West-Central Africa (Angola/Congo, ~40–50%; Luanda, Benguela, Cabinda, Loango ports → Brazil, Caribbean); Bight of Benin (Slave Coast, Dahomey/Oyo, ~15–20%; Ouidah, Lagos, Porto-Novo → Brazil, Saint-Domingue); Gold Coast (Asante, ~10–15%; Anomabo, Cape Coast, Elmina → Caribbean, North America); Senegambia (~5%; Gorée, Fort James); Southeast Africa (Mozambique, Kilwa, ~5–10% → Mascarene Islands, Brazil).
- **Economic impact**: Depopulation of interior; enrichment of coastal/middleman states (Dahomey, Asante, Lunda); firearms imports fueling further raiding; agricultural disruption.
- **Source**: [PRIMARY/DATABASE] Eltis & Richardson (2010) *Atlas of the Transatlantic Slave Trade* (Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database); [MONOGRAPH] Lovejoy (2012), Miller (1988), Law (1991), Sheriff (1987), Alpers (2009).

### Trans-Saharan Trade

- **Commodities**: Salt (Taoudenni mines → Timbuktu); gold (Bambuk, Bure → Maghreb); slaves (Sahel raids [Bornu, Hausa, Wadai] → Tripoli/Tunis → Ottoman Empire, ~5,000–10,000/year); kola nuts (forest zones → Hausaland, Sahel); textiles (North African cloth, European imports ↔ West African cotton cloth).
- **Key routes**: Timbuktu–Taoudenni–Morocco; Kano–Agadez–Tripoli (major commercial artery); Bornu–Fezzan–Tripoli; Darfur–Nile Valley–Cairo.
- **Decline**: Accelerates post-1800 (Atlantic trade dominance, Saharan insecurity, French colonial disruption 19th century).
- **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Austen (2010) *Trans-Saharan Africa in World History*.

### Gold Trade

- **Gold Coast** (Asante): Akan goldfields exported via European forts (Cape Coast, Elmina); some diverted to trans-Saharan routes. [MONOGRAPH] Wilks (1993) *Forests of Gold*.
- **Bambuk & Bure** (upper Senegal/Niger): Gold fields controlled by Bambara states, Manding; exported north across Sahara to Maghreb (declining by 1763 vs earlier centuries).
- **Zimbabwe Plateau** (Rozvi): Gold trade much reduced from earlier (Mutapa) era; Portuguese competition, trade-route shifts.

### Ivory Trade

- **East Africa**: Swahili coast (Kilwa, Mombasa) exported ivory to Oman, India, Persia, China; Yao and Makua hunters (interior). [MONOGRAPH] Sheriff (1987).
- **West-Central Africa**: Angola, Lunda/Luba regions; some ivory via Luanda/Benguela to Europe (secondary to slaves).
- **Southern Africa**: Trekboers, Xhosa, San hunted elephants; ivory exported via Cape or traded north.

### Cape Colony (VOC Economy)

- **Function**: Provisioning station for VOC ships (Dutch East Indies–Europe route).
- **Exports**: Wine (Constantia wine, sweet dessert wine, famous in Europe; produced by settlers, Simon van der Stel's estate 1685); wheat (for VOC ships, local consumption); meat (cattle, sheep from trekboer pastoralists, Khoikhoi trade/raiding).
- **Labor**: Slaves imported from Madagascar, East Africa (Mozambique), Indonesia (VOC territories). Khoikhoi dispossessed, employed as laborers; San marginalized.
- **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Penn (2005) *The Forgotten Frontier*; Ross (1999) *Status and Respectability in the Cape Colony*.

### Ethiopian Highlands

- **Economy**: Agriculture-based; coffee (native to Ethiopia, though major export era 19th–20th century), grains (teff, barley, wheat), livestock. Trade via Red Sea ports (Massawa—under Ottoman/Egyptian control, leased to Ethiopian emperors). Coffee to Yemen; slaves, ivory, hides exported. Internal trade (salt bars as currency).
- **Source**: [MONOGRAPH] Crummey (2000).
