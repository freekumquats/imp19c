# Research: Qing Salt Administration (鹽政) — officials, divisions, revenue, reform, design fit

Task #45 (backlog): was there a Qing salt inspector/official who oversaw the gabelle
monopoly? Feeds a possible salt-administration office/character alongside the Hoppo
(#111/#112) precedent. Answers Q1–Q5 below; sources listed at the end; unverifiable
items flagged.

## Q1. The hierarchy: 鹽政 vs 鹽運使 vs 鹽法道 vs governors-general

**巡鹽御史 / 鹽政 ("Salt Censor" → "Chief Salt Commissioner")** — the crux distinction.
- Formal title 巡視鹽政監督御史 ("Censor Sent to Supervise the Salt Administration").
  Nominal rank as a censor was only 5th grade, but the post was a **imperial commission
  (欽差)** — an emperor's personal delegate — which put it functionally above the
  3rd-grade Salt Controller it "supervised" (聽命 relationship inverted in practice:
  the 鹽運使 answered to the 鹽政, not vice versa). [zhidao.baidu 2267778370682933068;
  cross-checked against 清史稿/卷116 which lists 鹽運使 as 從三品 "以聽於鹽政"]
- Appointment channel: nominated by the 都察院 (Censorate) from among its censors and
  give-shi-zhong (給事中), then selected by the emperor. Term was nominally **one year**,
  officially "無定品" (no fixed rank attached to the post itself — it derived rank from
  the holder's substantive post). In the Kangxi–early Qianlong period actual tenures
  ran much longer than one year (Cao Yin/Li Xu rotated the Lianghuai post for a full
  decade, 1703–1712/1704–1713). [baidu zhidao; zh.wikipedia 曹寅]
- **Imperial Household Department (內務府) tie, NOT a formal subordination — a personnel
  pipeline.** The Lianghuai post in particular was overwhelmingly staffed by Manchu
  bondservants (包衣) of the Imperial Household Department, most famously Cao Yin (曹寅,
  father of Cao Xueqin's family, holder 1703–1712 alongside Li Xu 李煊) — simultaneously
  Jiangning Textile Commissioner (江寧織造). Ho Ping-ti states directly: "This coveted
  role typically went to a Manchu official from the Imperial Household Department," and
  that the post's title changed from **"salt censor" to "chief salt commissioner" in the
  1730s**, continuing until **1831/1832 when the merchant monopoly ended** (Tao Zhu's
  reform). [Ho Ping-ti 1954 via jstor 2718130, snippet]
  - Financial angle: Cao Yin/Li Xu's Lianghuai salt-censor tenure financed the Kangxi
    Southern Tours' entertainment and gifts directly out of Lianghuai salt-merchant
    money; a dissertation abstract on Lianghuai yanzheng states the post existed for
    **political, not economic, reasons** and that it "supported large sums of money for
    the Emperor's southern inspection tour and luxury from local salt taxation and salt
    merchants." Salt merchants "suffered exploitation from both government and the
    senior official (yanzheng)" and had to curry favor with him. [dissertationtopic.net
    /doc/1901801, abstract only — could not access full text, so holder-by-holder
    detail beyond Cao/Li is UNVERIFIED for this session]
  - The same abstract states Lianghuai's salt tax alone covered roughly **one-quarter of
    the entire state's income** at its administrative height — this is a strong claim
    from a single secondary abstract and should be treated as an upper-bound/rhetorical
    figure pending corroboration, not a hard number for the mod's revenue tuning.
  - Qianlong's Southern Tours were directly bankrolled by Yangzhou salt merchants: the
    1751 (first) tour is reported at "1 million taels" donated by Lianghuai salt
    merchants in exchange for increased permitted shipment weights per yin (引); the
    1757 tour saw the same merchants fund construction of an imperial lodge (天寧寺
    行宮) in Yangzhou. Six tours total, "over 2,000 attendants and 30+ imperial lodges,"
    with merchants — not the state treasury — bearing most costs. [multiple Chinese web
    sources, consistent across several independent write-ups, but all secondary/popular
    rather than primary-sourced — treat the "1 million taels" figure as approximate]

**都轉鹽運使司鹽運使 ("yunshi," Salt Controller/Transport Commissioner)** — the
substantive administrative head of a salt division.
- Rank: **從三品** (3rd grade, lower class) under the Qing. [zh.wikipedia 鹽運使;
  confirmed in 清史稿/卷116]
- Duties (verbatim from 清史稿 as rendered): "掌督察場民生計，商人行息，適時平其鹽價，
  管理水陸輸運" — i.e., oversee salt-yard producer livelihoods, merchant interest/credit,
  keep salt prices stable, and manage water/land transport of salt.
- Reported to the "督撫" (provincial governor-general/governor) who held concurrent
  salt-administration authority where no separate 鹽政 was posted, and — where a
  dedicated 鹽政 existed (Lianghuai above all) — nominally to that censor.
- Office was called 都轉鹽運使司 ("The Bureau of the Salt Controller"), with subordinate
  posts 運同 (從四品), 運副 (從五品), 監掣同知 (正五品), 運判 (從六品), etc.
- Historically, **7 permanent 鹽運使 posts**: 奉天 (Fengtian/Manchuria), 直隸 (Zhili),
  山東 (Shandong), 兩淮 (Lianghuai), 兩浙 (Liang-Zhe), 廣東 (Guangdong), 四川 (Sichuan).
  [zh.wikipedia 鹽運使 lists these 7, consistent across both English-summarized
  Wikipedia fetches]
- Historical churn: the Qing-era **河東 (Hedong/Shanxi) 鹽運使 was abolished in
  乾隆五十七年 (1792)**; the **福建 (Fujian) 鹽運使 was downgraded to a 鹽法道 in
  雍正四年 (1726)**. The office (both 鹽運使 and successor bureau) survived into the
  Republic and was only formally abolished in **1937**, replaced by 鹽務管理局
  (Salt Administration Bureau). [zh.wikipedia 鹽運使, corroborated twice]

**鹽法道 ("salt-law circuit intendant")** — used in provinces without a dedicated
鹽運使.
- Rank: **從三品** per one Chinese-Wikipedia rendering, but 清史稿/卷116 (fetched
  directly) instead gives 鹽法道 as **正四品**, one grade below the 鹽運使's 從三品 —
  the two sources disagree by one grade; treat rank as "roughly parallel to, one notch
  below, the yunshi" rather than a pinned number. [zh.wikipedia 鹽運使 vs 清史稿/卷116
  fetch — CONTRADICTION, flagged]
- Duties: "與鹽運使相同" (identical functions to the yunshi, just for smaller/second-tier
  salt zones).
- Deployment (per 清史稿/卷116): 江南, 江西, 福建, 湖北, 湖南, 河南, 山西, 陝西, 四川,
  廣西, 雲南 (one each), 甘肃 (two) — i.e., a much wider geographic spread than the 7
  yunshi seats, covering the smaller/inland salt-consuming provinces.

**Provincial governors-general holding salt duties concurrently (兼理/兼辦鹽政)** —
confirmed as a real, recurring pattern, though the mod-relevant instance is late:
- **長蘆 (Changlu, Tianjin) salt division**: the post of 直隸總督兼會辦鹽政
  ("Viceroy of Zhili concurrently handling salt administration") is independently
  documented (Baidu Baike entry of that exact title exists). Chinese secondary sources
  place the formal folding of Changlu salt administration into the Viceroy of Zhili's
  portfolio at **1860 (咸豐十年)** — i.e., this is a mid/late-19th-century consolidation,
  not a mid-Qianlong (1763) arrangement. [zh.wikipedia 直隸總督, 百度百科
  直隸總督兼會辦鹽政 — could not fetch full text of either, title + date corroborated
  only via search snippets, treat date as indicative not certain]
- A parallel arrangement is attested for **Fujian**, where the governor doubled as
  salt administrator after Fujian's 鹽運使 was downgraded to a 鹽法道 in 1726 — plausible
  given the downgrade, but I could not independently confirm the Fujian
  governor-concurrent-salt-duty claim beyond a search-result title fragment
  ("福建巡撫 兼盐政"). UNVERIFIED, flag for a follow-up primary-source check if this
  matters for a Fujian-specific mechanic.

**戶部 (Board of Revenue) vs 內務府 (Imperial Household Department) division of labor** —
this is the single most load-bearing distinction for the mod's design question, and the
sourcing is thinner than I'd like:
- The 鹽運使/鹽法道 track (substantive administration, price-setting, transport,
  merchant oversight) sat inside the **regular civil-service/provincial hierarchy**,
  ultimately reporting up through the governor-general to the Grand Council/throne, and
  its **revenue (鹽課) was booked through the 戶部** as ordinary state fiscal income —
  this is the ~5.7–7 million-tael-a-year stream discussed in Q3 below, is line-itemed
  in 清史稿·食貨志, and is uncontested in every source I found.
- The 鹽政/巡鹽御史 track (the Lianghuai post above all, and — per some sources — its
  Liang-Zhe and Changlu counterparts in the early-mid Qing) was staffed by Imperial
  Household Department personnel and functioned as a **quasi-private conduit for the
  emperor's privy purse and for underwriting imperial spectacle (the Southern Tours)**,
  parallel to — and structurally identical in spirit to — the Canton Hoppo (粵海關監督)
  arrangement the mod already models: an office nominally within the state apparatus,
  but staffed by 內務府 bondservants and financially oriented toward the throne's
  personal accounts rather than purely the 戶部 ledger.
- **Caveat**: I could not find a primary-source-grade statement that formally assigns
  "salt censor appointments" to 內務府 jurisdiction the way the Hoppo post is explicitly
  documented as an 內務府 appointment channel in the mod's own design notes (se_QING_CANTON.txt
  cites Morse directly for the Hoppo). For the salt censor, the 內務府 link rests on
  (a) the biographical fact that Cao Yin/Li Xu — attested 內務府 bondservants — held the
  Lianghuai post for a decade, and (b) Ho Ping-ti's summary characterization ("typically
  went to a Manchu official from the Imperial Household Department"). This is
  respectable secondary-source testimony but not as ironclad as the Hoppo's paper trail.
  Treat the 鹽政≈內務府-sinecure framing as **well-supported but not as airtight as the
  Hoppo case** — good enough to build a mechanic on, not good enough to claim as
  settled institutional law in-game flavor text without hedging ("often" not "always").

## Q2. The salt divisions (鹽區)

Confirmed division seats, mapped against yunshi/fazhidao deployment above:
- **兩淮 (Lianghuai)**, seat Yangzhou (揚州) — by consensus the largest and most
  lucrative division; supplied the lower Yangtze/Jiangnan heartland (~7 provinces per
  one source: 江蘇/安徽/江西/湖南/湖北/河南/貴州 region, figures vary by source).
  Administrative seat of both the 鹽運使 and (when posted) the 鹽政/巡鹽御史.
- **長蘆 (Changlu)**, seat originally elsewhere, **relocated to Tianjin in 1677**
  (康熙十六年運司移駐天津) — served the Zhili/Hebei coastal region.
- **兩浙 (Liang-Zhe)** — Zhejiang coastal salt, one of the 7 permanent yunshi seats.
- **山東 (Shandong)** — one of the 7 permanent yunshi seats, coastal Bohai-rim salterns.
- **河東 (Hedong, Shanxi)** — inland brine-well/lake salt (解池 Xie Lake basin), served
  Shanxi/Henan/Shaanxi; **its 鹽運使 post was abolished in 1792**, folded into a 鹽法道
  or provincial administration thereafter.
- **四川 (Sichuan)** — the deep brine-well country around **自貢 (Zigong)**; one of the
  7 permanent yunshi seats, but structurally distinct: production was dominated by
  **private, capital-intensive well-drilling enterprises** (the subject of Zelin's
  *Merchants of Zigong*) rather than the coastal evaporation-pan model of
  Lianghuai/Changlu/Liang-Zhe. Zigong salt served an unusually wide inland catchment —
  one source says Sichuan salt supplied "四川、湖南、湖北、貴州、雲南、甘肅六省及西藏"
  (six provinces plus Tibet), reflecting its position servicing landlocked inland China
  far from any coast.
- **福建 (Fujian)** — had a 鹽運使 downgraded to 鹽法道 status in 1726; supplied its own
  coast plus, per one source, "廣東、廣西、福建、江西、湖南、雲南、貴州七省" is actually
  attributed by a search snippet to **Guangdong's** catchment, not Fujian's — the
  agent-summarized search results garbled this pairing at least once; TREAT THE
  PROVINCE-TO-CATCHMENT MAPPING FROM SEARCH SNIPPETS AS ROUGH, not to be used verbatim
  for a mod province-list without a second check.
- **廣東 (Guangdong)** — one of the 7 permanent yunshi seats; per one (unverified in
  detail) source, the widest-reaching catchment of the coastal producers ("distributed
  across seven provinces": 廣東/廣西/福建/江西/湖南/雲南/貴州 per the same
  possibly-garbled snippet above).
- **雲南 (Yunnan)** — mentioned as a salt-consuming (and to a lesser extent
  salt-producing, from inland brine) province in several catchment lists above, but I
  did not find an independent statement of Yunnan having its own dedicated
  鹽運使/鹽法道 seat distinct from being served by Sichuan/Guangdong output — UNVERIFIED
  whether Yunnan had a standalone salt-division administration in the Qing; flag for
  follow-up if the mod wants a Yunnan-specific salt building/mechanic.
- **奉天 (Fengtian/Manchuria)** — the 7th permanent yunshi seat; served the
  Manchu homeland/Liaodong coast, low-fidelity relevance to the mod's China-fine-fidelity
  program unless the mod wants a Manchurian salt building.

**Largest by revenue/output**: every convergent line of evidence (the "1/4 of state
income" claim specifically about Lianghuai, the Yangzhou salt-merchant wealth described
in the Yangzhou Wikipedia article, Ho Ping-ti's whole monograph being *about* the
Yangzhou salt merchants specifically, and Tao Zhu's reform being staged at Lianghuai
rather than any other division) makes **兩淮/Lianghuai unambiguously the largest and
most important salt division**, by a wide margin, for the entire Qing period up to the
1832 reform. Changlu and Liang-Zhe are the next tier; Sichuan/Zigong is structurally
distinct (private capital, not state-monopoly gang system) and became increasingly
important in the 19th century as Lianghuai's system frayed and, especially, after the
Taiping Rebellion cut the Yangzi corridor (see Q5).

**Share of total state revenue**: one Chinese secondary source states salt tax
"長期占國家財政總收入的15%-20%，僅次於田賦" (long-term 15-20% of total state fiscal
revenue, second only to the land tax), rising to **~30% during the Taiping Rebellion
period** when land-tax collection collapsed in war zones. Total Qing state revenue
("歲入") in the mid-Qianlong period is given elsewhere as roughly **50 million taels**,
which would put the ~6-7 million-tael salt figure at **~12-14%** — broadly consistent
with, if slightly below, the "15-20%" characterization (that characterization likely
averages across the whole dynasty including higher-revenue later decades, and the "40
million taels" figure at the turn of the 19th century — which does NOT match the same
~50M total-revenue baseline — suggests either the 40M figure is for a much later/higher
year or is an error/mistranslation in that particular JSTOR snippet; I could not access
the full JSTOR article to resolve this discrepancy. **FLAG: the 40-million-tael salt
figure is inconsistent with every other figure gathered and should NOT be used without
finding the primary table in Production of Salt in China, 1644-1911.**).

The Tang-dynasty parallel figure ("天下之賦，鹽利其半" — salt = half of all state
revenue) is sometimes miscited as a Qing statement; it is from the **New Tang History**
(新唐書) describing the 8th-century Tang salt monopoly under Liu Yan, NOT the Qing.
Multiple Chinese web sources reuse the line loosely when discussing Qing salt without
being clear about its Tang origin — worth flagging since the team's brief's suggested
source list (Adshead) covers the Tang commission by the same "salt commissioner"
title-lineage; don't let "yantie shi" (Tang) and "yanzheng"/鹽政 (Qing) blur together.

## Q4. Merchants and the monopoly systems

**綱鹽法 (gang/syndicate franchise system)** — inherited from the late Ming (萌自明代牙行
行頭制度 per one 總商 encyclopedia entry — the 總商 institution "grew out of the Ming-era
broker-house-head [牙行行頭] system"). Under the gang system:
- The state fixed a limited quantity of 鹽引 (salt permits/vouchers) issued against
  registered merchant households organized into "gang books" (綱冊); only permit-holders
  could lawfully move and sell salt, within a fixed sales territory (引岸).
- Merchants paid tax (引課) to the 戶部 to obtain permits, then transported/sold within
  their assigned territory — a four-tier structure per one source (issue → collection →
  transport → retail).
- **總商 ("head merchants")** — a tier of super-merchants who held disproportionate
  permit allocations and functioned as a *quasi-official* layer: "總商不僅是商業領袖，還
  承擔著替官府催繳課稅、攤派捐輸、維持鹽區秩序的半官方職能" (head merchants were not just
  commercial leaders but bore the semi-official function of collecting tax arrears on
  the state's behalf, apportioning forced "contributions" (捐輸) among lesser merchants,
  and maintaining order within the salt zone). This is the group that financed the
  Southern Tours (Q1) and built the famous Yangzhou merchant gardens/estates (e.g. Ge
  Yuan, built by salt merchant Huang Zhiyun).
- The 總商 system declined after the **Xianfeng-era reforms** (i.e., post-Taiping,
  1850s-60s) and was progressively replaced by direct official tax collection through
  the Guangxu period, as the ticket system (below) and later likin displaced the gang
  franchise model.

**票鹽法 (ticket system) — Tao Zhu's 1832 Lianghuai reform**:
- **Tao Zhu (陶澍)**, Viceroy of Liangjiang (兩江總督) 1830–1839, launched the reform in
  the 5th lunar month of 1832 at **Huai-bei** (淮北, the northern half of the Lianghuai
  zone) specifically, in response to the hereditary merchant franchise's collapse: gang
  merchants were failing to move their contracted quotas while raising prices,
  smuggled/black-market salt sales had come to exceed legal sales, and government
  revenue was eroding.
- Mechanism: "任何信譽良好的商人都可以購買鹽票，用於購買大宗或小宗的單次貨運，可在商人
  希望的任何地方零售" — any merchant of good standing could buy a distribution ticket
  for a single shipment (large or small) and retail it wherever he wished; the tickets
  themselves were tradeable. This broke the hereditary-franchise/fixed-territory
  monopoly structure of the gang system.
- Effect: "opened the system up to small-scale traders, depriving the hereditary
  merchants and the smugglers of their profits and lowering costs to the consumer" —
  and this is also the point at which (per Ho Ping-ti) the Lianghuai **salt censor
  post itself was abolished (1831/32)**, since the post's institutional reason for
  being — administering the merchant-gang franchise on the throne's behalf — disappeared
  with the franchise.
- Limitation/nuance: Tao Zhu "could not meet the optimistic revenue commitments he had
  made to the emperor," and resigned in March 1839 — the reform improved distribution
  efficiency and consumer prices but did not fully solve the fiscal shortfall problem
  that had motivated it.
- Corroborating Chinese sources (无法完整读取，仅摘要): commemorative and academic pieces
  marking the "190th anniversary" of the reform (i.e. dated to 2022) frame it as
  "政府、商人與民眾" (government, merchants, and the populace) case study — these look
  like legitimate scholarly/semi-scholarly retrospectives but I could not fetch full
  text (site blocks) to extract further detail beyond the English-source summary above.

**Corruption/patronage as sinecure**: consistent across every source — the Lianghuai
salt-censor post (Cao Yin's tenure exemplifying it) ran on entangled patronage,
merchant "gift" money, forced contributions, and (per the Lianghuai-yanzheng
dissertation abstract) "exploitation from both government and the senior official" of
the merchants themselves. Ho Ping-ti frames the whole Yangzhou salt-merchant world as a
case study in "commercial capitalism" precisely because of this patronage-heavy,
politically-entangled structure rather than a clean free market.

## Q5. Chronology for the mod's windows

- **ca. 1763 (High Qianlong)** — the system is at/near its classic-form zenith: 鹽政
  (chief salt commissioner, terminology per Ho Ping-ti already shifted from "salt
  censor" in the 1730s) seated at Lianghuai, staffed by Imperial Household Department
  Manchu appointees, financially entangled with the throne's Southern Tours (the
  Qianlong tours ran 1751, 1757, 1762, 1765, 1780, 1784 — so 1763 sits between the 2nd
  and 3rd tours); 鹽運使 administering day-to-day production/pricing/transport at 7 major
  seats (incl. Lianghuai/Yangzhou, Changlu/Tianjin, Sichuan among others the mod already
  models); 綱鹽法 gang-merchant franchise and 總商 head-merchants intact and at their
  wealthiest; revenue running roughly 6-7.5 million taels/year, ~12-15% of total state
  income. **This is the mod's 1763 bookmark window — the system is in its classic,
  fully-functioning, patronage-heavy state.**
- **ca. 1815 (Jiaqing)** — no directly-sourced snapshot found this session, but by
  structural inference: the gang/總商 system is still nominally intact but showing the
  strain that would culminate in the 1832 crisis (declining merchant compliance with
  quotas, rising smuggling) — this is the well-documented lead-up condition to Tao
  Zhu's reform, not something I found a dedicated 1815-year figure for. UNVERIFIED
  point-in-time data for 1815 specifically; flag for a follow-up search focused on
  嘉慶 salt administration if the mod needs an 1815-specific number.
  1832 reform (Daoguang, not Jiaqing) is the real inflection point.
  post-Taiping likin evolution → 1913 foreign inspectorate — but exact intervening
  detail (mid-19th c. likin-on-salt mechanics, i.e. 鹽厘/鹽卡) is only lightly sourced
  this session (see below).
- **Post-Taiping (1850s-60s onward)**: the Taiping Rebellion (1851-1864) severed the
  Lianghuai salt-transport routes up the Yangzi, "the Treasury's reserves were depleted,
  central finances nearly paralyzed" (户部库存空虚，中央財政近乎癱瘓), which forced the
  Qing toward **likin (厘金)**, a transit-tax system, as an emergency substitute revenue
  stream when normal salt-tax collection became impossible in war-affected zones. This
  is the "likin on salt" the team's brief anticipated; I confirmed the *causal
  mechanism* (Taiping disruption → likin adoption generally) but did NOT find detailed
  sourcing on salt-specific likin administration mechanics (鹽厘/鹽卡 checkpoints) beyond
  this one summary — flag as a thin spot if the mod wants a specific late-Qing
  salt-likin mechanic.
  Also in this window: **1860 (咸豐十年)**, Changlu salt administration was folded into
  the concurrent portfolio of the Viceroy of Zhili (直隸總督兼會辦鹽政) — a real instance
  of the "governor-general holds salt duties concurrently" pattern from Q1, but dated to
  the 1860s, not 1763 — do not backdate this arrangement into the mod's 1763 bookmark.
- **1913 Reorganisation Loan / Richard Dane endpoint** — confirmed via multiple
  independent sources: **Sir Richard (Morris) Dane**, previously Inspector-General of
  Salt Revenue in British India (1907-1909), was brought in under the 1913
  Reorganisation Loan and became the founding figure of the **Sino-Foreign Salt
  Administration / Chief Inspectorate of the Central Salt Administration**, serving
  1913-1918. He built the new administration "on the Indian Civil Service model,
  recruiting district inspectors from many nationalities," and it "proved effective in
  gathering revenue" (secured as loan collateral). He co-directed the salt gabelle
  alongside a Chinese official, per a 1913 State Department historical document
  reference ("the Chinese Government has reluctantly consented to the plans of Sir
  Richard Dane, British co-director of the salt gabelle"). This is the clean **modern
  endpoint** of the institutional lineage the mod might want to reference in late-game
  flavor/decline text: from imperial-household sinecure (1763) → merchant-monopoly
  reform (1832) → post-Taiping fiscal improvisation (1850s-60s) → foreign-supervised
  modern bureau (1913+).

## Design recommendation (B)

**Yes — add a salt-administration officeholder character**, for the same reason the
Hoppo works: it is a single, historically real, revenue-relevant post whose *personal*
competence and corruption plausibly (and did, historically) swing a large, concrete
income stream, and the mod already has the building-level concreteness (Lianghuai/
Changlu/Sichuan salt yards, se_QING_BUILDINGS.txt) and the event (qing_revenue.1) that
currently treats the gabelle as abstract "minister fitness," with no character grading
its yield the way the Hoppo grades Canton customs.

**Recommended post: 兩淮鹽政 (Lianghuai Salt Censor / "Chief Salt Commissioner"),
English name suggestion: "Salt Commissioner" or "Superintendent of the Lianghuai Salt
Monopoly."**

Why this one specifically, not a "national" salt overseer:
- There was **no single national salt overseer** in the Qing system — the 鹽政 posts (where
  they existed at all, mainly Lianghuai and at times Liang-Zhe/Changlu) were regional,
  and most salt divisions were run only by a 鹽運使/鹽法道 answering to the provincial
  governor-general, with no separate censor above them. Inventing a unified
  "Minister of Salt" would be ahistorical in a way the mod's China-fine-fidelity
  principle argues against.
- Lianghuai is, by a wide margin, the historically dominant division (Q2/Q3) — it is
  the one division important enough, revenue-rich enough, and politically visible
  enough (Southern Tours financing, Cao Yin/Cao Xueqin-family lore, Ho Ping-ti's entire
  monograph) to justify a dedicated single character the way Canton customs justifies
  the Hoppo. The mod already privileges Lianghuai by seeding its salt yard first in
  se_QING_BUILDINGS.txt.
- It mirrors the Hoppo's institutional shape almost exactly: an Imperial Household
  Department-linked appointee (not always ironclad by the sourcing, but well-supported —
  see Q1 caveat), nominally outranking the career administrator beneath him (鹽運使,
  parallel to how the Hoppo outranks ordinary customs staff), financially tied to the
  throne's personal spending (Southern Tours ≈ the Hoppo's tribute-goods flow),
  and — crucially for gameplay — **historically abolished in 1831/32** when the
  gang-merchant system it administered was reformed away. That gives the mod a natural,
  historically-grounded end-of-life event/decision (a late-game "Reform the Lianghuai
  Salt Censorate" moment mirroring Tao Zhu, distinct from the existing abstract
  qing_revenue.1 reform/milk choice, which could instead become the mechanical
  expression of what happens when the player pulls that lever).

Suggested integration, concretely:
- Character title: **兩淮鹽政 (Liǎnghuái Yánzhèng)** — "Salt Commissioner of Lianghuai."
- Grade Lianghuai (and, by extension, mod-wide salt) yield/output on his **finesse**
  (administrative competence — merchant-quota compliance, price stability, revenue
  actually reaching the 戶部) and **corruption** (skim/patronage, mirroring the Hoppo's
  qing_hoppo_squeeze), the same two-axis pattern already proven in se_QING_CANTON.txt.
- Tie him directly to the three seeded salt-yard buildings (Lianghuai/Changlu/Sichuan)
  per backlog item #44, rather than leaving qing_revenue.1 abstract — e.g., his finesse
  factor scales output from `qing_salt_yard_building` provinces, exactly as
  `qing_canton_hoppo_factor` scales Canton trade income today.
- Appointment channel flavor: like the Hoppo, seat him as a 內務府-appointee character at
  game start (mirroring the #66 "seat a Hoppo at game start" fix) rather than leaving the
  post an empty abstraction the player never meets.
- Long-run hook: preserve room for a Tao-Zhu-style "abolish the salt censorate, go to
  ticket sales" decision later in the timeline (1815+ bookmark or a dynamic event),
  which would retire the character/office the same way the real 1831/32 reform did —
  consistent with the mod's on-map-object-lifecycle-symmetry rule (raise a real
  object → curtailing it must actually disband/retire it, not just leave a dangling
  variable).

## What could NOT be verified (explicit flags, gathered in one place)

1. The exact rank of 鹽法道 — 從三品 (zh.wikipedia summary) vs 正四品 (清史稿/卷116
   direct fetch) **contradict each other**; the primary-text fetch should be trusted
   over the tertiary summary, so treat 正四品 as more likely correct, but this wasn't
   independently triple-checked.
2. The "40 million taels at the turn of the [18th/19th] century" salt-revenue figure
   (JSTOR 2569253 snippet) is inconsistent with every other figure gathered (which
   cluster at 6-7.5M for mid-Qianlong and low millions for Kangxi) and could not be
   resolved — likely a different revenue category, a later date, or a
   snippet-truncation artifact. Do not use this figure without primary verification.
3. Whether the Imperial Household Department's link to the 鹽政 post was a *formal*
   appointment-jurisdiction rule (as it demonstrably was for the Hoppo) or merely an
   *informal, heavily-favored personnel pipeline* (which is what the actual evidence —
   Cao Yin/Li Xu's biographies plus Ho Ping-ti's "typically" phrasing — supports).
   Recommend hedging mod flavor text accordingly ("often staffed by," not "always
   controlled by").
4. Named holder of the Lianghuai 鹽政/鹽運使 post specifically at **1763** (the mod's
   bookmark year) — not found this session. Cao Yin died in 1712; there were multiple
   further holders across the 18th century whom I did not identify by name. If the mod
   wants a *historically named* starting officeholder (the way it presumably seats a
   real or plausible Hoppo), this needs a dedicated follow-up search, likely requiring
   access to 清實錄 or a dedicated biographical-dictionary source not reached this
   session.
5. 1815 (Jiaqing)-specific state of the salt administration — no dedicated
   point-in-time source found; inferred only by structural extrapolation between the
   1763 zenith and the 1832 crisis.
6. Salt-specific likin (鹽厘/鹽卡) mechanics post-Taiping — only the general
   Taiping-disruption-causes-likin-adoption mechanism was sourced, not the salt-specific
   checkpoint/levy details.
7. Fujian governor's concurrent salt-duty claim (Q1) — search-snippet-title level only.
8. Yunnan's status as an independent salt-producing/administering division vs. merely a
   salt-*consuming* catchment served by Sichuan/Guangdong output — unresolved.
9. S.A.M. Adshead's work (explicitly recommended in the task brief) — not located or
   consulted at all this session. This is the most important single gap, since Adshead
   is specifically the modernization-era (1900-1920) specialist and would likely
   resolve several of the above flags (especially the 1913 endpoint detail and possibly
   a cleaner revenue-percentage figure) better than the scattered sources actually used.
