## Q1. China-domestic price of tea vs Canton-export price — is there a GAP, and which way?

**What was found, and its limits.** No single clean "ordinary Chinese consumer paid X per catty"
figure with a solid citation was located this session (repeating the prior digest's finding).
The best datapoint is a low-confidence Baidu-sourced claim:

> "清朝茶叶价格差异很大，普通民茶每斤几钱银子，而贡茶或稀缺茶可能高达几十两" — "Qing tea prices
> varied enormously: ordinary commoner's tea cost several *qian* (十分之一兩, i.e. ~0.1-0.5
> taels) per *jin* (斤/catty), while tribute tea or rare varieties could reach dozens of
> taels [per jin]." — **Baidu AI-summary-tier source, NOT independently verified this session,
> flagged accordingly.**

If taken at face value, "several qian per jin" (~0.3-0.5 taels/catty, roughly) sits BELOW the
Canton-export Bohea range already established in the prior digest (12-20 taels/**picul** — note
unit: 1 picul = 100 catties, so 12-20 taels/picul = **0.12-0.20 taels/catty**). That is actually
in the SAME order of magnitude as the "several qian per jin" domestic figure, not obviously
below it — the domestic-vs-Canton-export GAP for **common/bulk tea specifically** may therefore
be much smaller than the earlier A0 framing implied, or may not exist at all for the coarsest
grades. This is an important qualification to the prior digest's headline claim.

**What is much better attested: grade stratification within China itself.**
- Bohea/Wuyi tea (the dominant Canton-export grade) was **not** a distinct "export-only" grade
  invented for foreigners — "Bohea" is simply the English rendering of Wuyi (武夷), the mountain
  region, and the term became a catch-all in the West for "common black tea" over the 18th
  century specifically because it WAS the common/bulk grade, sold in bulk at both ends (Wikipedia,
  "Bohea" article, cross-checked — moderate confidence, tertiary source but internally
  consistent with the primary-source EIC price data in the prior digest).
- Finer black-tea grades (Congou, Souchong, Pekoe) commanded a **visible price premium over
  Bohea at the SAME Canton export point**, not just after reaching Europe. The one full price
  table located and read this session — the House of Lords Journal's "Return of the Quantities
  and Prices of the several Sorts of Tea sold by The East India Company" (British History Online,
  read in full text, `british-history.ac.uk/lords-jrnl/vol62/pp1410-1411`) — covers 1814-1829,
  a half-century past the mod's bookmark, but shows the STRUCTURE clearly: Bohea was the
  cheapest (~1-2 shillings/lb, and the highest-VOLUME grade at 14.9-21 million lb/yr for Congou
  specifically), while Pekoe and Gunpowder ran 4-7 shillings/lb — a **~3-5× spread across grades
  at the same (British wholesale-auction) point in the chain**, not a domestic-vs-export split.
  This is EIC-sale-price data (post-voyage), not Canton price, so it cannot itself establish a
  China-side domestic/export gap — but it does establish that grade, not origin-market, is the
  primary axis of price variation once tea reaches any single point of sale.
- **The single most useful data point for the actual #52 question:** the earlier digest's own
  A2 table already shows Canton-export Bohea prices spanning **12-20 taels/picul across the whole
  1730-1770s record** (Van Dyke, read in full text) — i.e., even WITHIN the Canton-export market
  itself, before any voyage or European markup, prices varied by ~65% depending on year, contract
  terms (advance vs spot), and presumably grade. This is consistent with "tea pricing had a wide
  band even at origin" rather than "China-domestic = flat-cheap, export = flat-dear."

**Verdict for Q1, hedged:** the sharp CHEAP-DOMESTIC vs DEAR-EXPORT dichotomy the mod's design
hypothesis assumes is **not cleanly demonstrated** by what could be sourced this session for the
COMMON/bulk tea grade — the Canton-export price for bulk Bohea (0.12-0.20 taels/catty) and the
weakly-sourced domestic "several qian per jin" figure (~0.3-0.5 taels/catty) are close enough in
order of magnitude that the GAP, if real, is modest for the grade that dominated the actual
14M+ lb/yr export volume. The gap the mod should care about instead runs ACROSS GRADES (bulk
Bohea vs fine Congou/Pekoe/tribute tea), which existed on both the domestic AND export sides
simultaneously — see Q4.

## Q2. China-domestic price of silk (raw + cloth) vs Canton-export price

**No China-domestic raw-silk price-per-catty figure was located this session either**, repeating
the prior digest's gap (Kishimoto Mio's and Wu Chengming's/Chuan Han-sheng's relevant work was
identified by title/scope but not reached in full text — same access wall as before).

**However, one strong and directly-relevant NEW finding closes part of Q2/Q3 at once — the 1759
raw-silk export ban:**

> "乾隆二十四年為了平減市場絲價，乾隆帝下令禁止絲斤綢緞綢絹等物出口" — "In Qianlong 24 (1759), TO
> BRING DOWN MARKET SILK PRICES, the Qianlong Emperor ordered a ban on exporting raw silk,
> satins, and silk fabrics." — via a compiled secondary source hosted at docin.com
> (`www.docin.com/p-1121155238.html`), discussing 乾隆二十九年 (1764) silk-export archival
> records; **secondary/compiling source, not the primary edict text itself, moderate
> confidence.**
>
> Follow-up from the same source: the ban ran ~5 years and **did NOT bring domestic prices
> down** — "domestic silk prices did not actually decrease... when harvests were poor, prices
> still rose sharply" — while foreign merchants "求售" (kept demanding to buy) throughout,
> confirming the export demand was real and persistent, not the primary driver of the domestic
> price level (weather/harvest was).

This is a **historically real, dated (1759), motive-explicit case of the Qing state itself
believing EXPORT DEMAND was inflating DOMESTIC silk prices** — i.e., contrary to the mod's
export-premium hypothesis, at least one strand of 18th-century Qing economic policy explicitly
treated the causality as export-price-pressure-bleeding-INTO-the-domestic-market, not
"domestic cheap, export dear, no interaction." The ban's failure (prices didn't fall) suggests
the actual causal driver was supply-side (harvests), but the FRAMING itself is a data point
against a clean domestic/export firewall.

**Grade-tiered export control, partially sourced:** a related Qing-era institution,
丝绸出口定限 (the silk-export quota system), reportedly restricted foreign merchants to
**low-grade Huzhou silk (低档湖丝)** only, with smuggling **top-grade Huzhou silk (上等湖丝)**
above 100 catties punished by military conscription — per a Baidu-tier secondary source,
**low confidence, could not verify the primary regulation text or exact date this session.**
If accurate, this is the silk-sector mirror of the tea-grade point in Q1/Q4: the BEST silk
(上等湖丝) was reserved, by policy, for a market other than the export trade — strongly
suggestive of a domestic elite/luxury silk tier the export price never touches, which is exactly
the counter-case Q4 asks about. Flagged for follow-up verification, not treated as settled.

**RAW vs FINISHED distinction (the mod's own RAW_SILK vs SILK_CLOTH split):** no source located
this session gives a clean raw-silk-vs-cloth price ratio for China domestically. The general
economic logic that RAW_SILK (an intermediate input) sits well below SILK_CLOTH (a finished
luxury good) in ANY 18th-century economy is not silk-specific and was not separately verified
here — it is standard input-vs-finished-good economics, not something requiring a fresh cite,
but no numeric ratio for Qing China specifically was found.

## Q3. Where did the dearness come from? — decomposing the China→Europe escalation

Building on the prior digest's A0 figures, one important NEW figure closes a real gap in the
distribution-side accounting:

**British import duty on tea, pre-1784, was ~119% of value** — reduced to 12.5% by Pitt's
Commutation Act of 1784 (multiple corroborating tertiary sources, moderate-high confidence for
the 119%→12.5% figures specifically, as they recur consistently and the causal story — duty cut
killed the smuggling trade "virtually overnight" — is a well-known point in British fiscal
history). **Separately corroborated**, a History News Network source states smuggled
(duty-free) tea sold at roughly **half the price of legal (duty-paid) tea** pre-1784 — consistent
with a duty in the 100%+ range, since removing that duty from the price should roughly halve it.
This means, for the specific market of Georgian Britain, **import duty ALONE — before any
Company margin, before any retailer's cut — could double the landed cost of tea.** This sits
ON TOP OF, not instead of, the previously-documented Canton-to-London value chain (voyage-risk
premium, the Company's own ~150% gross margin per Dermigny, the 2.4-3.4× De Vries/Melitz
Cape-route sales-to-purchase ratio).

**Decomposition, restated with this new figure layered in:**
1. Canton wholesale (Chinese-merchant-to-foreign-buyer): the base, ~12-20 taels/picul for bulk
   Bohea (prior digest, Van Dyke).
2. Voyage risk (bottomry): junk ~40%/voyage, European ship ~12-18%/voyage (prior digest, Van
   Dyke Table 2.2) — priced INTO freight/insurance cost before landing.
3. Company resale margin: ~150% (2.5×) gross, per the Dermigny 1729-1741 sixteen-vessel sample
   (prior digest).
4. **British import duty specifically: ~119% pre-1784** (this session, new) — a THIRD large
   multiplicative layer, distinct from and in addition to the Company's own margin (duty was
   paid to the Crown, not pocketed by the Company).
5. Retailer/grocer markup on top of the duty-paid wholesale price: **not quantified in any
   source located this session** (repeated search failure) — the single biggest remaining gap
   in the distribution-side accounting.

**Verdict on Q3:** every additional layer this session found (voyage risk, Company margin, THE
NEW duty figure) is a DISTRIBUTION-side cost, external to China and external to the underlying
commodity. The weight of sourced evidence continues to say the MAJORITY of the China→Europe
price escalation is distributional, not a China-domestic luxury premium baked into the base
commodity. This corroborates, rather than overturns, the prior digest's A0 finding — Q3 is a
CONFIRM of the export-premium thesis, with one new concrete number (119% British duty) added to
the stack.

## Q4. The counter-case — was there ALSO a genuine domestic Chinese luxury tier?

This is where this session's findings most meaningfully complicate the mod's simple
"export-premium-only" framing.

**Tribute tea (貢茶) and gentry/connoisseur tea markets were real and price-differentiated,**
though hard evidence for a specific multiple is thin:
- Emperor Kangxi is documented distributing Biluochun as a reward to elderly officials at state
  banquets (multiple tertiary sources, consistent, moderate confidence) — tribute tea functioned
  as a status good circulated through imperial/gentry channels, NOT sold on an open market at
  all, which itself means it sits outside any single "price" concept.
- The weakly-sourced Baidu figure in Q1 ("贡茶或稀缺茶可能高达几十两" — tribute/rare tea up to
  dozens of taels per jin) would, if true, put fine domestic tea at **~100-400× the "several
  qian" ordinary-tea figure from the SAME low-confidence source** — a genuinely enormous
  domestic-only spread, independent of export at all. This is the single most important
  unresolved number in this whole digest: if even roughly correct, it means China had its OWN
  luxury-tier tea market with a spread comparable to or larger than the entire Canton-to-London
  value chain, entirely internal to China and untouched by the export trade.
- Wuyi rock tea's (武夷岩茶) reputation as a Qing-literati luxury item is asserted by tertiary
  sources but **no price figure could be sourced this session** to quantify it.

**The silk-grade-reservation finding from Q2 (上等湖丝 restricted from export, low-grade only
sold to foreigners)** is structurally the strongest single piece of evidence for a genuine
domestic luxury tier: if policy reserved the BEST silk for a non-export market, that market
must have been paying for it — almost certainly the domestic elite/court/weaving-bureau economy
(Jiangning/Suzhou/Hangzhou imperial weaving bureaus were confirmed to exist and to serve the
court specifically, per general sources, though no price premium for their output over
commercial cloth could be quantified this session).

**Nanjing brocade (雲錦) as a modern proxy, heavily caveated:** modern hand-woven yunjin retails
at ~800-8,800+ RMB/meter versus ~200-800 RMB/meter for machine-imitation cloth — a >10×, and at
the high end >40×, spread. This is a MODERN retail figure for a modern artisanal-heritage
product, NOT a period 1763 price, and is included here only as illustrative of the
"寸錦寸金" (an inch of brocade, an inch of gold) idiom's persistence — **flagged explicitly as
NOT usable as a period figure, included for color only, not for the mod's base_value math.**

**Verdict on Q4:** the counter-case is real but the digest cannot currently attach solid numbers
to it. The strongest evidence (silk-grade export reservation; the tribute-tea Baidu figure; the
1759 export-ban episode) all point the same direction — **yes, there was a domestic Chinese
luxury tier for both tea and silk, running parallel to and independent of the export trade** —
but every one of these is sourced at "moderate" or "low" confidence by this session's tools, and
none produces a clean, citable multiple that could be dropped into the mod's base_value math
with confidence. This is the single most important honest gap in this digest.

## Q5. VERDICT for #52

**PARTIAL — neither a clean confirm nor a clean disprove. The export-premium framing is
correct about WHERE MOST OF THE MEASURED PRICE ESCALATION LIVES, but incomplete as a total
explanation, because a separate domestic luxury tier almost certainly also existed and is not
well quantified.**

Specifically:

1. **Q3's finding (distribution-side decomposition) CONFIRMS the mod's export-premium
   framing for the CANTON-TO-EUROPE leg specifically.** Every additional cost layer found this
   session and in the prior digest — Canton wholesale margin variance, voyage/bottomry risk,
   Company resale margin (~150%), and now British import duty (~119% pre-1784) — is
   distributional, external to the underlying Chinese commodity, and stacks multiplicatively.
   **This part of the mod's design assumption is well-supported: an export/tradezone-side
   premium mechanic (Hoppo-style graded modifier, per the prior digest's Section E
   recommendation) is the right place to model the Canton→Europe escalation.**

2. **Q1/Q2's finding (the domestic-vs-Canton-export gap for BULK/common-grade tea and silk
   specifically) is WEAKER than the mod's hypothesis assumes, maybe absent.** The one
   apples-to-apples comparison this session could construct (bulk Bohea's own Canton-export
   price vs a low-confidence domestic-common-tea figure) puts the two in the SAME order of
   magnitude, not "cheap in China, dear at the point of export." If this holds up under better
   sourcing, it would mean the mod should NOT assume its bulk/common tea and silk base_value is
   automatically "too high" just because export existed — the Canton-export price for the BULK
   grade may already be close to a reasonable China-domestic bulk price.

3. **Q4's finding (a domestic Chinese luxury tier for fine tea and fine silk, running in
   PARALLEL to, not instead of, the export trade) is the piece the mod's simple two-region
   framing (China-domestic-cheap vs export-dear) MISSES entirely.** The 1759 silk-export-ban
   episode, the silk-grade export reservation (low-grade-only to foreigners), and the tribute-tea
   price-spread claim all point toward China having its OWN internal luxury/staple split for
   these exact goods — a split the export trade did not create and was not the primary channel
   for. If the mod wants "luxuries should be dear," a **grade-tiered domestic luxury lever
   (tribute/fine tea, fine silk/court-textile grade) is likely ALSO needed, separately from
   any export/tradezone premium** — the two are not substitutes for each other, they explain
   different halves of the same commodity's price range.

**Concrete recommendation for #52, given this partial verdict:** do NOT resolve #52 by choosing
only ONE lever (export-premium OR domestic-luxury-tier). The sourced evidence supports BOTH
existing simultaneously and independently: (a) an export/tradezone-side additive premium
modifier for goods leaving China through Canton (per the prior digest's Section E, reusing the
Hoppo-modifier pattern), representing the well-documented Canton-to-Europe distributional
escalation; AND (b) if the mod wants to represent tribute tea / fine silk / court-grade textile
consumption as a distinct in-game tier at all, that needs its OWN domestic-grade multiplier,
NOT modeled as a side-effect of the export mechanic, because the sourced evidence (weak as it
is) suggests this domestic tier predates and is independent of the export trade. Given the
current confidence levels, (a) is ready to design against now; (b) needs a follow-up research
pass with real primary-source or database access (the same three-scholar wall — Kishimoto Mio,
Wu Chengming, Chuan Han-sheng — flagged repeatedly across this research program) before a
specific domestic-luxury multiple could be defended in a design doc.
